DEI VERBUM
CONSTITUTIO DOGMATICA DE DIVINA REVELATIONE
DOGMATIC CONSTITUTION ON DIVINE REVELATION
DEI VERBUM religiose audiens et fidenter proclamans, Sacrosancta Synodus verbis S. Ioannis obsequitur dicentis: Adnuntiamus vobis vitam aeternam, quae erat apud Patrem et apparuit nobis: quod vidimus et audivimus adnuntiamus vobis, ut et vos societatem habeatis nobiscum, et societas nostra sit cum Patre et cum Filio eius Iesu Christo (1Jn 1,2-3). Propterea, Conciliorum Tridentini et Vaticani I inhaerens vestigiis, genuinam de divina revelatione ac de eius transmissione doctrinam proponere intendit, ut salutis praeconio mundus universus audiendo credat, credendo speret, sperando amet.
2 It pleased God in His goodness and wisdom to reveal Himself and to make known the mystery of His will (cf. Eph 1:9), by which men through Christ, the Word made flesh, in the Holy Spirit have access to the Father and are made partakers of the divine nature (cf. Eph 2:18; 2 Pet 1:4). By this revelation, therefore, the invisible God (cf. Col 1:15; 1 Tim 1:17) from the abundance of His love speaks to men as friends (cf. Ex 33:11; Jn 15:14-15) and lives among them (cf. Bar 3:38), that He may invite and receive them into fellowship with Himself. This economy of revelation is made by deeds and words intrinsically connected with each other, so that the works, accomplished by God in the history of salvation, manifest and confirm the doctrine and the realities signified by the words, and the words proclaim the works and elucidate the mystery contained in them. Moreover, the intimate truth both of God and of the salvation of man shines forth in Christ through this revelation, who is at once the mediator and the fullness of all revelation.
¶333
CAPUT I. DE IPSA REVELATIONE
CHAPTER I. ON REVELATION ITSELF
Placuit Deo in sua bonitate et sapientia Seipsum revelare et notum facere sacramentum voluntatis suae (cf. Ep 1,9), quo homines per Christum, Verbum carnem factum, in Spiritu Sancto accessum habent ad Patrem et divinae naturae consortes efficiuntur (cf. Ep 2,18 Ep 2 Petr. 1, 4). Hac itaque revelatione Deus invisibilis (cf. Col 1,15 1Tm 1,17) ex abundantia caritatis suae homines tamquam amicos alloquitur (cf. Ex 33,11 Jn 15,14-15) et cum eis conversatur (cf. Ba 3,38), ut eos ad societatem Secum invitet in eamque suscipiat. Haec revelationis oeconomia fit gestis verbisque intrinsece inter se connexis, ita ut opera, in historia salutis a Deo patrata, doctrinam et res verbis significatas manifestent ac corroborent, verba autem opera proclament et mysterium in eis contentum elucident. Intima autem per hanc revelationem tam de Deo quam de hominis salute veritas nobis in Christo illucescit, qui mediator simul et plenitudo totius revelationis exsistit.
3 God, creating and conserving all things through the Word (cf. Jn 1:3), gives a perpetual testimony of Himself to men in created things (cf. Rom 1:19-20), and, intending to open the way of supernal salvation, moreover manifested Himself to the first parents from the beginning. After their fall, He raised them up in the hope of salvation by promising redemption (cf. Gen 3:15), and He took care of the human race without ceasing, that He might give eternal life to all who seek salvation by patience in good work (cf. Rom 2:6-7). In His own time, He called Abraham, to make him into a great nation (cf. Gen 12:2-3), which after the Patriarchs He instructed through Moses and the prophets to acknowledge Himself the only living and true God, a provident Father and just judge, and to await the promised Savior, and thus through the ages He prepared the way for the Gospel.
¶334
Deus, per Verbum omnia creans (cf. Jn 1,3) et conservans, in rebus creatis perenne sui testimonium hominibus praebet (cf. Rm 1,19-20) et, viam salutis supernae aperire intendens, insuper protoparentibus inde ab initio Semetipsum manifestavit. Post eorum autem lapsum eos, redemptione promissa, in spem salutis erexit (cf. Gn 3,15), et sine intermissione generis humani curam egit, ut omnibus qui secundum patientiam boni operis salutem quaerunt, vitam aeternam daret (cf. Rm 2,6-7). Suo autem tempore Abraham vocavit, ut faceret eum in gentem magnam (cf. Gn 12,2-3), quam post Patriarchas per Moysen et Prophetas erudivit ad se solum Deum vivum et verum, providum Patrem et iudicem iustum agnoscendum, et ad promissum Salvatorem expectandum, atque ita per saecula viam Evangelio praeparavit.
4 After God spoke in many and various ways through the prophets, "lastly in these days has spoken to us in the Son" (Heb 1:1-2). For He sent His Son, the eternal Word, who enlightens all men, to dwell among men and tell them of the intimate things of God (cf. Jn 1:1-18). Jesus Christ, therefore, the Word made flesh, a man sent to men, "speaks the words of God" (Jn 3:34), and completes the saving work which the Father gave Him to do (cf. Jn 5:36; 17:4). Therefore He Himself, whom to see is to see the Father (cf. Jn 14:9), completes and perfects revelation, confirming it by divine testimony, by His whole presence and manifestation, by words and works, by signs and miracles, especially by His death and glorious resurrection from the dead, and finally by sending the Spirit of truth, namely that God is with us to free us from the darkness of sin and death and to raise us up to eternal life. The Christian economy, therefore, as the new and definitive covenant, will never pass away, and no new public revelation is to be expected before the glorious manifestation of our Lord Jesus Christ (cf. 1 Tim 6:14; Tit 2:13).
¶335
Postquam vero multifariam multisque modis Deus locutus est in Prophetis, novissime diebus istis locutus est nobis in Filio (He 1,1-2). Misit enim Filium suum, aeternum scilicet Verbum, qui omnes homines illuminat, ut inter homines habitaret iisque intima dei enarraret (cf. Jn 1,1-18). Iesus Christus ergo, Verbum caro factus? homo ad homines missus, "verba Dei loquitur" (Jn 3,34), et opus salutare consummat quod dedit ei Pater faciendum (cf. Jn 5,36 Jn 17,4). Quapropter Ipse, quem qui videt, videt et Patrem (cf. Jn 14,9), tota suiipsius praesentia ac manifestatione, verbis et operibus, signis et miraculis, praesertim autem morte sua et gloriosa ex mortuis resurrectione, misso tandem Spiritu veritatis, revelationem complendo perficit ac testimonio divino confirmat, Deum nempe nobiscum esse ad nos ex peccati mortisque tenebris liberandos et in aeternam vitam resuscitandos. Oeconomia ergo christiana, utpote foedus novum et definitivum, numquam praeteribit et nulla iam nova revelatio publica expectanda est ante gloriosam manifestationem Domini nostri Iesu Christi (cf. 1Tm 6,14 cf. Tt 2,13).
5 To God revealing is to be rendered the obedience of faith (cf. Rom 16:26; cf. Rom 1:5; 2 Cor 10:5-6), by which man commits his whole self freely to God, rendering full submission of intellect and will to God revealing and willingly assenting to the revelation given by Him. That this faith may be given, it is necessary that the grace of God prevenient and assisting and the internal aids of the Holy Spirit, who moves the heart and converts it to God, opens the eyes of the mind, and gives to all sweetness in consenting to and believing the truth. So that the understanding of revelation may become ever deeper, the same Holy Spirit continually perfects faith by His gifts.
¶336
Deo revelanti praestanda est oboeditio fidei (cf. Rm 16 Rm 26 coll. Rm 1,5 2Co 10,5-6), qua homo se totum libere Deo committit plenum revelanti Deo intellectus et voluntatis obsequium praestando et voluntarie revelationi ab Eo datae assentiendo. Quae fides ut praebeatur, opus est praeveniente et adiuvante gratia Dei et internis Spiritus Sancti auxiliis, qui cor moveat et in Deum convertat, mentis oculos aperiat, et det omnibus suavitatem in consentiendo et credendo veritati. Quo vero profundior usque evadat revelationis intelligentia, idem Spiritus Sanctus fidem iugiter per dona sua perficit.
6 By divine revelation God wished to manifest and communicate Himself and the eternal decrees of His will concerning the salvation of men, namely to share divine goods, which entirely surpass the understanding of the human mind. The sacred Synod confesses that God, the beginning and end of all things, can be certainly known by the natural light of human reason from created things (cf. Rom 1:20); but it teaches that it is to be attributed to His revelation that those things which in divine matters are not of themselves impervious to human reason can, in the present condition of the human race, be known by all with ease, with firm certainty, and with no admixture of error.
¶337
Divina revelatione Deus Seipsum atque aeterna voluntatis suae decreta circa hominum salutem manifestare ac communicare voluit, ad participanda scilicet bona divina. quae humanae mentis intelligentiam omnino superant. Confitetur Sacra Synodus, Deum, rerum omnium principium et finem, naturali humanae rationis lumine e rebus creatis certo cognosci posse (cf. Rm 1,20); eius vero revelationi tribuendum esse docet, ut ea, quae in rebus divinis humanae rationi per se impervia non sunt. In praesenti quoque generis humani conditione ab omnibus expedite, firma certitudine et nullo admixto errore cognosci possint.
7 God most benignly arranged that those things which He had revealed for the salvation of all nations should remain whole forever and be transmitted to all generations. Therefore Christ the Lord, in whom the whole revelation of the most high God is consummated (cf. 2 Cor 1:30; 3:16—4:6), gave mandate to the Apostles that they should preach to all the Gospel, which promised before through the prophets He Himself fulfilled and promulgated with His own mouth, as the source of all saving truth and moral discipline, communicating divine gifts to them. Which indeed was faithfully done, both by the Apostles, who in oral preaching, examples, and institutions handed down those things which they had received either from the mouth, conversation, and works of Christ, or had learned from the suggestion of the Holy Spirit, and by those Apostles and apostolic men, who, under the inspiration of the same Holy Spirit, committed the message of salvation to writing.
¶338
CAPUT II. DE DIVINAE REVELATIONIS TRANSMISSIONE
CHAPTER II. ON THE TRANSMISSION OF DIVINE REVELATION
Quae Deus ad salutem cunctarum gentium revelaverat, eadem benignissime disposuit ut in aevum integra permanerent omnibusque generationibus transmitterentur. Ideo Christus Dominus, in quo summi Dei tota revelatio consummatur (cf. 2Co 1,32 cf. 2Co 3,16-4,6), mandatum dedit Apostolis ut Evangelium, quod promissum ante per Prophetas Ipse adimplevit et proprio ore promulgavit, tamquam fontem omnis et salutaris veritatis et morum disciplinae omnibus praedicarent, eis dona divina communicantes. Quod quidem fideliter factum est, tum ab Apostolis, qui in praedicatione orali, exemplis et institutionibus ea tradiderunt quae sive ex ore, conversatione et operibus Christi acceperant, sive a Spiritu Sancto suggerente didicerant, tum ab illis Apostolis virisque apostolicis, qui, sub inspiratione eiusdem Spiritus Sancti, nuntium salutis scriptis mandaverunt.
That the Gospel might be kept whole and living always in the Church, the Apostles left bishops as their successors, handing over to them their own place of teaching. Therefore this sacred Tradition and the sacred Scripture of both Testaments are as a mirror in which the Church pilgrimaging on earth contemplates God, from whom she receives all things, until she is led to see Him face to face as He is (cf. 1 Jn 3:2).
¶339
Ut autem Evangelium integrum et vivum iugiter in Ecclesia servaretur, Apostoli successores reliquerunt Episcopos, ipsis suum ipsorum locum magisterii tradentes. Haec igitur Sacra Traditio et Sacra utriusque Testamenti Scriptura veluti speculum sunt in quo Ecclesia in terris peregrinans contemplatur Deum, a quo omnia accipit, usquedum ad Eum videndum facie ad faciem sicuti est perducatur (cf. 1Jn 3,2).
8 Therefore apostolic preaching, which is expressed in a special way in the inspired books, was to be preserved by a continuous succession until the end of time. Hence the Apostles, handing down what they themselves had received, warn the faithful to hold the traditions which they have learned either by word or by letter (cf. 2 Th 2:15), and to contend for the faith once delivered to the saints (cf. Jude 3). But what has been handed down by the Apostles encompasses all those things which contribute to leading the life of the People of God holy and to increasing faith, and thus the Church, in her doctrine, life, and worship, perpetuates and transmits to all generations all that she herself is, all that she believes.
¶340
Itaque praedicatio apostolica, quae in inspiratis libris speciali modo exprimitur, continua successione usque ad consummationem temporum conservari debebat. Unde Apostoli, tradentes quod et ipsi acceperunt, fideles monent ut teneant traditiones quas sive per sermonem sive per epistulam didicerint (cf. 2Th 2,15), utque pro semel sibi tradita fide decertent (cf. Iud. 3) . Quod vero ab Apostolis traditum est, ea omnia complectitur quae ad Populi Dei vitam sancte ducendam fidemque augendam conferunt, sicque Ecclesia, in sua doctrina, vita et cultu, perpetuat, cunctisque generationibus transmittit omne quod ipsa est, omne quod credit.
This tradition which is from the Apostles, under the assistance of the Holy Spirit, progresses in the Church: for there grows the perception of the things handed down, both of words and realities, both from the contemplation and study of believers, who compare these things in their heart (cf. Lk 2:19, 51), and from the intimate understanding of spiritual things which they experience, and from the preaching of those who, with the succession of the episcopate, have received the certain charism of truth. The Church, namely, through the rolling ages, continually tends to the fullness of divine truth, until the words of God are consummated in her.
¶341
Haec quae est ab Apostolis Traditio sub assistentia Spiritus Sancti in Ecclesia proficit: crescit enim tam rerum quam verborum traditorum perceptio, tum ex contemplatione et studio credentium, qui ea conferunt in corde suo (cf. Lc 2,19 Lc 2,51), tum ex intima spiritualium rerum quam experiuntur intelligentia, tum ex praeconio eorum qui cum episcopatus successione charisma veritatis certum acceperunt. Ecclesia scilicet, volventibus saeculis, ad plenitudinem divinae veritatis iugiter tendit, donec in ipsa consummentur verba Dei.
The sayings of the holy Fathers testify to the life-giving presence of this Tradition, whose riches are poured into the practice and life of the believing and praying Church. Through the same Tradition the whole canon of the sacred books is known to the Church, and the sacred scriptures themselves are more deeply understood and unceasingly made active; thus God, who spoke of old, converses without ceasing with the Spouse of His beloved Son, and the Holy Spirit, through whom the living voice of the Gospel resounds in the Church, and through her in the world, leads believers into all truth and makes the word of Christ abundantly dwell in them (cf. Col 3:16).
¶342
Sanctorum Patrum dicta huius Traditionis vivificam testificantur praesentiam, cuius divitiae in praxim vitamque credentis et orantis Ecclesiae transfunduntur. Per eandem Traditionem integer Sacrorum Librorum canon Ecclesiae innotescit, ipsaeque Sacrae Litterae in ea penitius intelliguntur et indesinenter actuosae redduntur; sicque Deus, qui olim locutus est, sine intermissione cum dilecti Filii sui Sponsa colloquitur, et Spiritus Sanctus, per quem viva vox Evangelii in Ecclesia, et per ipsam in mundo resonat, credentes in omnem veritatem inducit, verbumque Christi in eis abundanter inhabitare facit (cf. Col 3,16).
9 Sacred Tradition, therefore, and sacred Scripture are closely connected and communicate with each other. For both, flowing from the same divine source, coalesce in a certain way into one and tend to the same end. For sacred Scripture is the speech of God as it is consigned to writing under the inspiration of the divine Spirit; but sacred Tradition transmits the word of God, entrusted by Christ the Lord and the Holy Spirit to the Apostles, wholly to their successors, that they, under the light of the Spirit of truth, may faithfully keep, expound, and spread it by their preaching; whence it follows that the Church does not derive her certainty concerning all revealed things from sacred Scripture alone. Wherefore, both are to be received and venerated with equal affection of piety and reverence.
¶343
Sacra Traditio ergo et Sacra Scriptura arcte inter se connectuntur atque communicant. Nam ambae, ex eadem divina scaturigine promanantes, in unum quodammodo coalescunt et in eundem finem tendunt. Etenim Sacra Scriptura est locutio Dei quatenus divino afflante Spiritu scripto consignatur; Sacra autem Traditio verbum Dei, a Christo Domino et a Spiritu Sancto Apostolis concreditum, successoribus eorum integre transmittit, ut illud, praelucente Spiritu veritatis, praeconio suo fideliter servent, exponant atque diffundant; quo fit ut Ecclesia certitudinem suam de omnibus revelatis non per solam Sacram Scripturam hauriat. Quapropter utraque pari pietatis affectu ac reverentia suscipienda et veneranda est.
10 Sacred Tradition and sacred Scripture constitute one sacred deposit of the word of God entrusted to the Church, adhering to which the whole holy people, united to its pastors, continually perseveres in the doctrine of the Apostles, in communion, in the breaking of bread, and in prayers (cf. Acts 2:42), so that in holding, exercising, and professing the faith handed down, there is a singular conspiracy of the bishops and the faithful.
¶344
Sacra Traditio et Sacra Scriptura unum verbi Dei sacrum depositum constituunt Ecclesiae commissum, cui inhaerens tota plebs sancta Pastoribus suis adunata in doctrina Apostolorum et communione, fractione panis et orationibus iugiter perseverat (cf. Ac 2,42 gr.), ita ut in tradita fide tenenda, exercenda profitendaque singularis fiat Antistitum et fidelium conspiratio.
The office of authentically interpreting the written or handed down word of God has been entrusted only to the living Magisterium of the Church, whose authority is exercised in the name of Jesus Christ. Which Magisterium is not above the word of God, but serves it, teaching only what has been handed down, insofar as it piously hears, holy guards, and faithfully expounds it by divine mandate and with the assistance of the Holy Spirit, and draws from this one deposit of faith all those things which it proposes to be believed as divinely revealed. It is clear, therefore, that sacred Tradition, sacred Scripture, and the Magisterium of the Church, according to the most wise design of God, are so connected and associated that one does not stand without the others, and all together, each in its own way, under the action of the one Holy Spirit, effectively contribute to the salvation of souls.
¶345
Munus autem authentice interpretandi verbum Dei scriptum vel traditum soli vivo Ecclesiae Magisterio concreditum est, cuius auctoritas in nomine Iesu Christi exercetur. Quod quidem Magisterium non supra verbum Dei est, sed eidem ministrat, docens nonnisi quod traditum est, quatenus illud, ex divino mandato et Spiritu Sancto assistente, pie audit, sancte custodit et fideliter exponit, ac ea omnia ex hoc uno fidei deposito haurit quae tamquam divinitus revelata credenda proponit. Patet igitur Sacram Traditionem, Sacram Scripturam et Ecclesiae Magisterium, iuxta sapientissimum Dei consilium, ita inter se connecti et consociari, ut unum sine aliis non consistat, omniaque simul, singula suo modo sub actione unius Spiritus Sancti, ad animarum salutem efficaciter conferant.
11 Divinely revealed things, which are contained and set forth in sacred Scripture, have been consigned under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit. For holy Mother Church, from the apostolic faith, holds the books of both the Old and New Testament, with all their parts, as sacred and canonical, because, having been written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit (cf. Jn 20:31; 2 Tim 3:16; 2 Pet 1:19-21; 3:15-16), they have God as their author, and as such have been handed down to the Church itself. But in composing the sacred books, God chose men, whom He used with their own faculties and powers, so that, He acting in them and through them, they, as true authors, should consign to writing all and only those things which He himself willed. Since, therefore, all that the inspired authors or hagiographers assert should be held as asserted by the Holy Spirit, it is to be professed that the books of Scripture teach firmly, faithfully, and without error that truth which God willed to be consigned to the sacred letters for the sake of our salvation. Thus "all Scripture, divinely inspired, is useful for teaching, for reproving, for correcting, for instructing in justice: that the man of God may be perfect, furnished to every good work" (2 Tim 3:16-17).
¶346
CAPUT III. DE SACRAE SCRIPTURAE DIVINA INSPIRATIONE ET DE EIUS INTERPRETATIONE
CHAPTER III. ON THE DIVINE INSPIRATION OF SACRED SCRIPTURE AND ON ITS INTERPRETATION
Divinitus revelata, quae in Sacra Scriptura litteris continentur et prostrant, Spiritu Sancto afflante consignata sunt. Libros enim integros tam Veteris quam Novi Testamenti, cum omnibus eorum partibus, sancta Mater Ecclesia ex apostolica fide pro sacris et canonicis habet, propterea quod, Spiritu Sancto inspirante conscripti (cf. Jn 20,31 2Tm 3,16 2P 1,19-21 2P 3,15-16), Deum habent auctorem, atque ut tales ipsi Ecclesiae traditi sunt. In sacris vero libris conficiendis Deus homines elegit, quos facultatibus ac viribus suis utentes adhibuit, ut Ipso in illis et per illos agente, ea omnia eaque sola, quae Ipse vellet, ut veri auctores scripto traderent. Cum ergo omne id, quod auctores inspirati seu hagiographi asserunt, retineri debeat assertum a Spiritu Sancto, inde Scripturae libri veritatem, quam Deus nostrae salutis causa Litteris Sacris consignari voluit, firmiter, fideliter et sine errore docere profitendi sunt. Itaque omnis Scriptura divinitus inspirata et utilis ad docendum, ad arguendum, ad corripiendum, ad erudiendum in iustitia: ut perfectus sit homo Dei, ad omne opus bonum instructus (2Tm 3,16-17, gr.) .
12 Since, however, God in sacred Scripture spoke through men in a human way, the interpreter of sacred Scripture, to understand what He wished to communicate to us, should attentively investigate what the hagiographers really intended to signify and what it pleased God to manifest by their words. To ascertain the intention of the hagiographers, among other things, regard must be paid to literary genera. For truth is proposed and expressed differently in texts variously historical, or prophetic, or poetic, or in other kinds of speech. Moreover, it is necessary that the interpreter seek that sense which the hagiographer, in given circumstances, according to the condition of his time and his culture, using the literary genera then in use, intended and expressed. For to rightly understand what the sacred author wished to assert in writing, due attention must be paid both to the accustomed and native ways of feeling, speaking, and narrating which flourished in the time of the hagiographer, and to those which were commonly used in the mutual commerce of men at that age.
¶347
Cum autem Deus in Sacra Scriptura per homines more hominum locutus sit, interpres Sacrae Scripturae, ut perspiciat, quid Ipse nobiscum communicare voluerit, attente investigare debet, quid hagiographi reapse significare intenderint et eorum verbis manifestare Deo placuerit. Ad hagiographorum intentionem eruendam inter alia etiam genera litteraria respicienda sunt. Aliter enim atque aliter veritas in textibus vario modo historicis, vel propheticis, vel poeticis, vel in aliis dicendi generibus proponitur et exprimitur. Oportet porro ut interpres sensum inquirat, quem in determinatis adiunctis hagiographus, pro sui temporis et suae culturae condicione, ope generum litterariorum illo tempore adhibitorum exprimere intenderit et expresserit. Ad recte enim intelligendum id quod sacer auctor scripto asserere voluerit, rite attendendum est tum ad suetos illos nativos sentiendi, dicendi, narrandive modos, qui temporibus hagiographi vigebant, tum ad illos qui illo aevo in mutuo hominum commercio passim adhiberi solebant.
But, since sacred Scripture is to be read and interpreted by the same Spirit through whom it was written, to rightly draw out the sense of the sacred texts, no less diligent attention is to be paid to the content and unity of the whole of Scripture, regard being had to the living tradition of the whole Church and the analogy of faith. It is the part of exegetes, according to these rules, to labor to more deeply understand and expound the sense of sacred Scripture, that by study, as it were prepared, the judgment of the Church may ripen. For all these things concerning the manner of interpreting Scripture ultimately are subject to the judgment of the Church, who fulfills the divine mandate and ministry of keeping and interpreting the word of God.
¶348
Sed, cum Sacra Scriptura eodem Spiritu quo scripta est etiam legenda et interpretanda sit, ad recte sacrorum textuum sensum eruendum, non minus diligenter respiciendum est ad contentum et unitatem totius Scripturae, ratione habita vivae totius Ecclesiae Traditionis et analogiae fidei. Exegetarum autem est secundum has regulas adlaborare ad Sacrae Scripturae sensum penitius intelligendum et exponendum, ut quasi praeparato studio, iudicium Ecclesiae maturetur. Cuncta enim haec, de ratione interpretandi Scripturam, Ecclesiae iudicio ultime subsunt, quae verbi Dei servandi et interpretandi divino fungitur mandato et ministerio.
13 In sacred Scripture, therefore, the saving truth and sanctity of God always being preserved, the admirable condescension of eternal Wisdom is manifested, that we may learn the ineffable benignity of God, and how great condescension of speech He used, having care and providence for our nature. For the words of God, expressed in human languages, have been made like to human speech, just as the Word of the eternal Father, having assumed the flesh of human weakness, was made like to men.
¶349
In Sacra Scriptura ergo manifestatur, salva semper Dei veritate et sanctitate, aeternae Sapientiae admirabilis condescensio, ut discamus ineffabilem Dei benignitatem, et quanta sermonis attemperatione usus sit, nostrae naturae providentiam et curam habens. Dei enim verba, humanis linguis expressa, humano sermoni assimilia facta sunt, sicut olim Aeterni Patris Verbum, humanae infirmitatis assumpta carne, hominibus simile factum est.
14 The most loving God, solicitously intending and preparing the salvation of the whole human race, by a singular dispensation chose for Himself a people, to whom He would entrust promises. For having entered into a covenant with Abraham (cf. Gen 15:18) and with the people of Israel through Moses (cf. Ex 24:8), He so revealed Himself to His acquired people as the one true and living God by words and deeds, that Israel might experience what the ways of God with men were, and, God Himself speaking through the mouth of the prophets, might daily understand them more deeply and more clearly and set them forth more widely among the nations (cf. Ps 21:28-29; 95:1-3; Is 2:1-4; Jer 3:17). The economy of salvation, however, foretold, narrated, and explained by the sacred authors, stands as the true word of God in the books of the Old Testament; wherefore these books, divinely inspired, retain perpetual value: "For whatever things have been written, have been written for our doctrine, that through the patience and consolation of the Scriptures we may have hope" (Rom 15:4).
¶350
CAPUT IV. DE VETERE TESTAMENTO
CHAPTER IV. ON THE OLD TESTAMENT
Amantissimus Deus totius humani generis salutem sollicite intendens et praeparans, singulari dispensatione populum sibi elegit, cui promissiones concrederet. Foedere enim cum Abraham (cf. Gn 15,18) et cum plebe Israel per Moysen (cf. Ex 24,8) inito, populo sibi acquisito ita Se tamquam unicum Deum verum et vivum verbis ac gestis revelavit, ut Israel, quae divinae essent cum hominibus viae experiretur, easque, ipso Deo per os Prophetarum loquente, penitius et clarius in dies intellegeret atque latius in gentes exhiberet (cf. Ps 21,28-29 Ps 95,1-3 Is 2,1-4 Jr 3,17). Oeconomia autem salutis ab auctoribus sacris praenuntiata, enarrata atque explicata, ut verum Dei verbum in libris Veteris Testamenti exstat; quapropter hi libri divinitus inspirati perennem valorem servant: Quaecumque enim scripta sunt, ad nostram doctrinam scripta sunt, ut per patientiam et consolationem Scripturarum spem habeamus (Rm 15,4).
15 The economy of the Old Testament was principally disposed for this, that it might prepare, prophetically foretell (cf. Lk 24:44; Jn 5:39; 1 Pet 1:10), and signify by various types (cf. 1 Cor 10:11) the coming of Christ the universal Redeemer and of the Messianic Kingdom. The books of the Old Testament, however, according to the condition of the human race before the time of salvation established by Christ, manifest to all both the knowledge of God and of man, and the ways in which the just and merciful God acts with men. Which books, although they also contain imperfect and temporary things, nevertheless show true divine pedagogy. Hence the same books, which express the living sense of God, in which sublime doctrines concerning God and saving wisdom concerning human life are hidden, and wonderful treasures of prayers, in which finally the mystery of our salvation lies hidden, are to be devoutly received by Christ's faithful.
¶351
Veteris Testamenti oeconomia ad hoc potissimum disposita erat, ut Christi universorum redemptoris Regnique Messianici adventum praeparet, prophetice nuntiaret (cf. Lc 24,44 Jn 5,39 1P 1,10) et variis typis significaret (cf. 1Co 10,11). Veteris autem Testamenti libri, pro condicione humani generis ante tempora instauratae a Christo salutis, Dei et hominis cognitionem ac modos quibus Deus iustus et misericors cum hominibus agit, omnibus manifestant. Qui libri, quamvis etiam imperfecta et temporaria contineant, veram tamen paedagogiam divinam demonstrant. Unde iidem libri, qui vivum sensum Dei exprimunt, in quibus sublimes de Deo doctrinae ac salutaris de vita hominis sapientia mirabilesque precum thesauri reconduntur, in quibus tandem latet mysterium salutis nostrae, a Christifidelibus devote accipiendi sunt.
16 God, therefore, the inspirer and author of the books of both Testaments, so wisely disposed that the New lies hidden in the Old, and the Old is made manifest in the New. For, although Christ established the New Covenant in His blood (cf. Lk 22:20; 1 Cor 11:25), yet the books of the Old Testament, whole and entire, are taken up into the proclamation of the Gospel, and in the New Testament acquire and show their complete meaning (cf. Mt 5:17; Lk 24:27; Rom 16:25-26; 2 Cor 3:14-16), and in turn illuminate and explain it.
¶352
Deus igitur librorum utriusque Testamenti inspirator et auctor, ita sapienter disposuit, ut Novum in Vetere lateat et in Novo Vetus pateat. Nam, etsi Christus in sanguine suo Novum Foedus condidit (cf. Lc 22,20 1Co 11,25) libri tamen Veteris Testamenti integri in praeconio evangelico assumpti, in Novo Testamento significationem suam completam acquirunt et ostendunt (cf. Mt 5,17 Lc 24,27 Rm 16,25-26 2Co 3,14-16), illudque vicissim illuminant et explicant.
17 The word of God, which is the power of God for salvation to every believer (cf. Rom 1:16), is presented and shows its power in an excellent way in the writings of the New Testament. For when the fullness of time came (cf. Gal 4:4), the Word was made flesh and dwelt among us full of grace and truth (cf. Jn 1:14). Christ restored the Kingdom of God on earth, manifested His Father and Himself by deeds and words, and completed His work by death, resurrection, and glorious ascension, and the mission of the Holy Spirit. Lifted up from the earth, He draws all to Himself (cf. Jn 12:32), He who alone has the words of eternal life (cf. Jn 6:68). This mystery, however, was not made known to other generations as it has now been revealed to His holy Apostles and Prophets in the Holy Spirit (cf. Eph 3:4-6), that they might preach the Gospel, arouse faith in Jesus Christ as Lord, and gather the Church. Of these things the writings of the New Testament stand as a perpetual and divine testimony.
¶353
CAPUT V. DE NOVO TESTAMENTO
CHAPTER V. ON THE NEW TESTAMENT
Verbum Dei, quod virtus Dei est in salutem omni credenti (cf. Rm 1,16), in scriptis Novi Testamenti praecellenti modo praesentatur et vim suam exhibet. Ubi enim venit plenitudo temporis (cf. Ga 4,4), Verbum caro factum est et habitavit in nobis plenum gratiae et veritatis (cf. Jn 1,14). Christus Regnum Dei in terris instauravit, factis et verbis Patrem suum ac Seipsum manifestavit, atque morte, resurrectione et gloriosa ascensione missioneque Spiritus Sancti opus suum complevit. Exaltatus a terra omnes ad Seipsum thahit (cf. Jn 12,32, gr.), Ipse qui solus verba vitae aeternae habet (cf. Jn 6,68). Hoc autem mysterium aliis generationibus non est patefactum, sicut nunc revelatum est sanctis Apostolis Eius et Prophetis in Spiritu Sancto (cf. Ep 3,46, gr.), ut Evangelium praedicarent, fidem in Iesum Christum ac Dominum excitarent et Ecclesiam congregarent. Quarum rerum scripta Novi Testamenti exstant testimonium perenne atque divinum.
18 It is not unknown to anyone that among all the Scriptures of the New Testament, the Gospels justly excel, as being the principal testimony concerning the life and doctrine of the incarnate Word, our Savior. The Church has always and everywhere held and holds that the four Gospels have apostolic origin. For what the Apostles by mandate of Christ preached, afterwards, the Holy Spirit inspiring, they themselves and apostolic men handed down to us in writing as the foundation of faith, namely the fourfold Gospel according to Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John.
¶354
Neminem fugit inter omnes, etiam Novi Testamenti Scripturas, Evangelia merito excellere, quippe quae praecipuum testimonium sint de Verbi Incarnati, Salvatoris nostri, vita atque doctrina. Quattuor Evangelia originem apostolicam habere Ecclesia semper et ubique tenuit ac tenet. Quae enim Apostoli ex mandato Christi praedicaverunt, postea divino afflante Spiritu, In scriptis, ipsi et apostolici viri nobis tradiderunt fidei fundamentum, quadriforme nempe Evangelium, secundum Matthaeum, Marcum, Lucam et Ioannem.
19 Holy Mother Church has firmly and most constantly held and holds that the four listed Gospels, whose historicity she unhesitatingly affirms, faithfully hand down what Jesus the Son of God, living among men, really did and taught for their eternal salvation, until the day on which He was taken up (cf. Acts 1:1-2). The Apostles, after the Lord's ascension, handed down to their hearers those things which He had said and done with a fuller understanding, which they, instructed by the glorious events of Christ and taught by the light of the Spirit of truth, enjoyed. The sacred authors, however, wrote the four Gospels, selecting some things from the many handed down by word or already in writing, reducing some to a synthesis, explaining some in view of the condition of the churches, always retaining the form of proclamation, that they might communicate to us true and sincere things concerning Jesus. For they wrote with that intention, whether from their own memory and recollection, or from the testimony of those who from the beginning were eyewitnesses and ministers of the word, that we might know the truth concerning those things in which we have been instructed (cf. Lk 1:2-4).
¶355
Sancta Mater Ecclesia firmiter et constantissime tenuit ac tenet quattuor recensita Evangelia, quorum historicitatem incunctanter affirmat, fideliter tradere quae Iesus Dei Filius, vitam inter homines degens, ad aeternam eorum salutem reapse fecit et docuit, usque in diem qua assumptus est (cf. Ac 1,1-2). Apostoli quidem post ascensionem Domini, illa quae Ipse dixerat et fecerat, auditoribus ea pleniore intelligentia tradiderunt, qua ipsi, eventibus gloriosis Christi instructi et lumine Spiritus veritatis edocti, fruebantur. Auctores autem sacri quattuor Evangelia conscripserunt, quaedam e multis aut ore aut iam scripto traditis seligentes, quaedam in synthesim redigentes, vel statui ecclesiarum attendendo explanantes, formam denique praeconii retinentes, ita semper ut vera et sincera de Iesu nobiscum communicarent. Illa enim intentione scripserunt, sive ex sua propria memoria et recordatione, sive ex textimonio illorum qui ad initio ipsi viderunt et ministri fuerunt sermonis, ut cognoscamus eorum verborum de quibus eruditi sumus, veritatem (cf. Lc 1,2-4).
20 The canon of the New Testament, besides the four Gospels, also contains the epistles of St. Paul and other apostolic writings written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, by which, according to the wise design of God, those things concerning Christ the Lord are confirmed, His genuine doctrine is more and more declared, the saving power of the divine work of Christ is preached, the beginnings and wonderful diffusion of the Church are narrated and its glorious consummation foretold. For to His Apostles the Lord Jesus, as He had promised, was present (cf. Mt 28:20), and sent to them the Paraclete Spirit, who would lead them into the fullness of truth (cf. Jn 16:13).
¶356
Novi Testamenti canon praeter quattuor Evangelia etiam epistulas sancti Pauli aliaque scripta apostolica Spiritu Sancto inspirante exarata continet, quibus, ex sapienti Dei consilio, ea quae sunt de Christo Domino confirmantur, genuina Eius doctrina magis magisque declaratur, salutifera virtus divini operis Christi praedicatur, Ecclesiae initia ac admirabilis diffusio narrantur eiusque consummatio gloriosa praenuntiatur. Apostolis enim suis Dominus Iesus sicut promiserat affuit (cf. Mt 28,20) et iis Paraclitum Spiritum misit, qui eos in plenitudinem veritatis induceret (cf. Jn 16,13).
21 The Church has always venerated the divine Scriptures just as she has venerated the Lord's body itself, since, especially in the sacred Liturgy, she does not cease to take and to offer to the faithful the bread of life from the table both of the word of God and of the body of Christ. She has always held and holds them, together with sacred Tradition, as the supreme rule of her faith, since, being inspired by God and consigned to writing once for all, they impart the word of God Himself unchangeably, and cause the voice of the Holy Spirit to resound in the words of the Prophets and Apostles. Therefore all ecclesiastical preaching, just as the Christian religion itself, must be nourished and ruled by sacred Scripture. For in the sacred books the Father who is in heaven meets His sons most lovingly and confers with them; and such great force and power is in the word of God that it stands as the support and vigor of the Church, and for the sons of the Church as strength of faith, food of the soul, a pure and perpetual source of spiritual life. Hence the saying excellently applies to sacred Scripture: "For the word of God is living and efficient" (Heb 4:12), which is powerful to build up and to give inheritance among all the sanctified (Acts 20:32; cf. 1 Th 2:13).
¶357
CAPUT VI. DE SACRA SCRIPTURA IN VITA ECCLESIAE
CHAPTER VI. ON SACRED SCRIPTURE IN THE LIFE OF THE CHURCH
Divinas Scripturas sicut et ipsum Corpus dominicum semper venerata est Ecclesia, cum, maxime in sacra Liturgia, non desinat ex mensa tam verbi Dei quam Corporis Christi panem vitae sumere atque fidelibus porrigere. Eas una cum Sacra Traditione semper ut supremam fidei suae regulam habuit et habet, cum a Deo inspiratae et semel pro semper litteris consignatae, verbum ipsius Dei immutabiliter impertiant, atque in verbis Prophetarum Apostolorumque vocem Spiritus Sancti personare faciant. Omnis ergo praedicatio ecclesiastica sicut ipsa religio christiana Sacra Scriptura nutriatur et regatur oportet. In sacris enim libris Pater qui in caelis est filiis suis peramanter occurrit et cum eis sermonem confert; tanta autem verbo Dei vis ac virtus inest, ut Ecclesiae sustentaculum ac vigor, et Ecclesiae filiis fidei robur, animae cibus, vitae spiritualis fons purus et perennis exstet. Unde de Sacra Scriptura excellenter valent dicta: Vivus est enim sermo Dei et efficax (He 4,12), qui potens est aedificare et dare hereditatem in sanctificatis omnibus (Ac 20,32 cf. 1Th 2,13).
22 Access to sacred Scripture must be widely open to Christ's faithful. Wherefore the Church from the beginning received as her own that most ancient Greek version of the Old Testament called the Septuagint; she also always holds in honor other Eastern versions and Latin versions, especially that called the Vulgate. But since the word of God must be available at all times, the Church with motherly solicitude takes care that suitable and correct translations be made into various languages, especially from the primary texts of the sacred books. Which, if, given the opportunity and the authority of the Church approving, they be made with common effort also with our separated brothers, can be used by all Christians.
¶358
Christifidelibus aditus ad Sacram Scripturam late pateat oportet. Qua de causa Ecclesia inde ab initiis graecam illam antiquissimam Veteris Testamenti versionem a LXX viris nuncupatam ut suam suscepit; alias vero versiones orientales et versiones latinas, praecipue illam Vulgatam vocant, semper in honore habet. Cum autem verbum Dei omnibus temporibus praesto esse debeat, Ecclesia materna sollicitudine curat, ut aptae ac rectae exarentur in varias linguas versiones, praesertim ex primigenis Sacrorum Librorum textibus. Quae si, data opportunitate et annuente Ecclesiae auctoritate, conficiantur communi etiam cum fratribus seiunctis nisu, ab omnibus christianis adhiberi poterunt.
23 The Spouse of the incarnate Word, the Church namely, taught by the Holy Spirit, strives to attain to a deeper understanding of the sacred Scriptures day by day, that she may unceasingly feed her sons on divine words; wherefore she also rightly fosters the study of the holy Fathers both of the East and West and of the sacred Liturgies. Catholic exegetes, however, and other cultivators of sacred theology, with sedulously joined forces, ought to labor, under the vigilance of the sacred Magisterium, to investigate and set forth the divine Scriptures with opportune aids, that as many ministers of the divine word as possible may be able fruitfully to supply the nourishment of the Scriptures to the people of God, which enlightens the mind, strengthens the will, and inflames the hearts of men to the love of God. The sacred Synod gives heart to the sons of the Church, cultivators of biblical matters, that they may continue to carry out the work happily undertaken, with renewed strength day by day, with all study according to the sense of the Church.
¶359
Verbi incarnati Sponsa, Ecclesia nempe, a Sancto Spiritu edocta, ad profundiorem in dies Scripturarum Sacrarum intelligentiam assequendam accedere satagit, ut filios suos divinis eloquiis indesinenter pascat; quapropter etiam studium sanctorum Patrum tum Orientis tum Occidentis et sacrarum Liturgiarum rite fovet. Exegetae autem catholici, aliique Sacrae Theologiae cultores, collatis sedulo viribus, operam dent oportet, ut sub vigilantia Sacri Magisterii, aptis subsidiis divinas Litteras ita investigent et proponant, ut quam plurimi divini verbi administri possint plebi Dei Scripturarum pabulum fructuose suppeditare, quod mentem illuminet, firmet voluntates, hominum corda ad Dei amorem accendat. Sacra Synodus Ecclesiae filiis, biblicarum rerum cultoribus, animum addit, ut opus feliciter susceptum, renovatis in dies viribus, omni studio secundum sensum Ecclesiae exsequi pergant.
24 Sacred theology rests on the written word of God, together with sacred Tradition, as on a perpetual foundation, and in it it is most firmly strengthened and always grows young, investigating all truth hidden in the mystery of Christ under the light of faith. The sacred Scriptures, however, contain the word of God and, because they are inspired, are truly the word of God; therefore the study of sacred page is to be as the soul of sacred theology. By the same word of Scripture, moreover, the ministry of the word, namely pastoral preaching, catechesis, and all Christian instruction, in which the liturgical homily must hold an excellent place, is healthfully nourished and holy grows strong.
¶360
Sacra Theologia in verbo Dei scripto, una cum Sacra Traditione, tamquam in perenni fundamento innititur, in eoque ipsa firmissime roboratur semperque iuvenescit, omnem veritatem in mysterio Christi conditam sub lumine fidei perscrutando. Sacrae autem Scripturae verbum Dei continent et, quia inspiratae, vere verbum Dei sunt; ideoque Sacrae Paginae studium sit veluti anima Sacrae Theologiae. Eodem autem Scripturae verbo etiam ministerium verbi, pastoralis nempe praedicatio, catechesis omnisque instructio christiana, in qua homilia liturgica eximium locum habeat oportet, salubriter nutritur sancteque virescit.
25 Wherefore, all clerics, especially the priests of Christ and others who as deacons or catechists lawfully attend to the ministry of the word, must adhere to the Scriptures by assiduous sacred reading and careful study, lest any of them become an empty outward preacher of the word of God, who is not a hearer from within, while he ought to communicate the most ample riches of the divine word, especially in the sacred Liturgy, with the faithful entrusted to him. Likewise the holy Synod strongly and especially exhorts all Christ's faithful, especially religious, that by frequent reading of the divine Scriptures they learn the eminent knowledge of Jesus Christ (Phil 3:8). For ignorance of the Scriptures is ignorance of Christ. Therefore let them gladly approach the sacred text itself, whether through the sacred Liturgy, rich in divine words, or through pious reading, or through institutions apt for this and other aids, which, the pastors of the Church approving and taking care, are laudably spread everywhere in our time. Let them be mindful, however, that prayer should accompany the reading of sacred Scripture, that it may become a conversation between God and man; for we speak to Him when we pray; we hear Him when we read the divine oracles.
¶361
Quapropter clericos omnes, imprimis Christi sacerdotes ceterosque qui ut diaconi vel catechistae ministerio verbi legitime instant, assidua lectione sacra atque exquisito studio in Scripturis haerere necesse est, ne quis eorum fiat verbi Dei inanis forinsecus praedicator, qui non est intus auditor, dum verbi divini amplissimas divitias, speciatim in sacra Liturgia, cum fidelibus sibi commissis communicare debet. Pariter Sancta Synodus christifideles omnes, praesertim sodales religiosos, vehementer peculiariterque exhortatur, ut frequenti divinarum Scripturarum lectione eminentem scientiam Iesu Christi (Ph 3,8) ediscant. Ignoratio enim Scripturarum ignoratio Christi est. Libenter igitur ad sacrum textum ipsum accedant, sive per sacram Liturgiam divinis eloquiis confertam, sive per piam lectionem, sive per institutiones ad id aptas aliaque subsidia, quae approbantibus et curantibus Pastoribus Ecclesiae ubique nostro tempore laudabiliter diffunduntur. Meminerint autem orationem concomitari debere Sacrae Scripturae lectionem, ut fiat colloquium inter Deum et hominem; nam illum alloquimur, cum oramus; illum audimus, cum divina legimus oracula.
It pertains to the sacred Bishops, in whom the apostolic doctrine resides, opportunely to instruct the faithful entrusted to them for the right use of the divine books, especially of the New Testament and above all the Gospels, through translations of the sacred texts, which are furnished with necessary and truly sufficient explanations, that the sons of the Church may safely and usefully converse with the sacred Scriptures and be imbued with their spirit. Moreover, editions of sacred Scripture, furnished with apt annotations, for the use also of non-Christians and accommodated to their conditions, are to be prepared, which the pastors of souls or Christians of whatever state may wisely take care to spread.
¶362
Sacris autem Antistitibus, apud quos est apostolica doctrina, fideles sibi commissos ad rectum divinorum libro rum usum, praesertim Novi Testamenti et imprimis Evangeliorum, opportune instituere competit per sacrorum textuum versiones, quae necessariis et vere sufficientibus explicationibus instructae sint, ut tuto ac utiliter Ecclesiae filii cum Scripturis Sacris conversentur earumque spiritu imbuantur. Insuper editiones Sacrae Scripturae, aptis instructae adnotationibus, ad usum etiam non-christianorum eorundemque conditionibus accommodatae, conficiantur, quas quoquomodo sive animarum Pastores sive Christiani cuiuscumque status spargere sapienter curent.
26 Thus, through the reading and study of the sacred books, the word of God may run and be glorified (2 Th 3:1), and the treasure of revelation, entrusted to the Church, may more and more fill the hearts of men. Just as from the assiduous frequentation of the eucharistic mystery the life of the Church receives increase, so a new impulse of spiritual life may be hoped from increased veneration of the word of God, which remains forever (Is 40:8; 1 Pet 1:23-25).
¶363
Ita ergo lectione et studio Sacrorum Librorum sermo Dei currat et clarificetur (2Th 3,1), thesaurusque revelationis, Ecclesiae concreditus, magis magisque corda hominum impleat. Sicut ex assidua frequentatione mysterii Eucharistici Ecclesiae vita incrementum suscipit, ita novum spiritualis vitae impulsum sperare licet ex adaucta veneratione verbi Dei, quod manet in aeternum (Is 40,8 1P 1,23-25).
1 Men of our age are becoming more and more conscious of the dignity of the human person, and the number of those who require that men in acting enjoy their own counsel and responsible freedom, moved not by coercion but by the sense of duty, increases. Likewise they demand a juridical delimitation of public power, lest the bounds of legitimate freedom of both the person and of associations be too circumscribed. This exigency of freedom in human society chiefly respects those things which are of the human spirit, especially those which pertain to the free exercise of religion in society. Attentively considering these aspirations of minds, and intending to declare how much they are conformable to truth and justice, this Vatican Synod examines the sacred tradition and doctrine of the Church, from which it ever brings forth new things congruent with old.
¶364
DIGNITATIS HUMANAE
DECLARATIO DE LIBERTATE RELIGIOSA
DECLARATION ON RELIGIOUS LIBERTY
De iure personae et communitatum ad libertatem socialem et civilem in re religiosa
ON THE RIGHT OF PERSONS AND COMMUNITIES TO SOCIAL AND CIVIL FREEDOM IN MATTERS RELIGIOUS
DIGNITATIS HUMANAE personae homines hac nostra aetate magis in dies conscii fiunt, atque numerus eorum crescit qui exigunt, ut in agendo homines proprio suo consilio et libertate responsabili fruantur et utantur, non coercitione commoti, sed officii conscientia ducti. Itemque postulant iuridicam delimitationem potestatis publicae, ne fines honestae libertatis et personae et associationum nimis circumscribantur. Quae libertatis exigentia in societate humana ea maxime respicit quae sunt animi humani bona, imprimis quidem ea quae liberum in societate religionis exercitium spectant. Ad has animorum appetitiones diligenter attendens, sibique proponens declarare quantum sint veritati et iustitiae conformes, haec Vaticana Synodus sacram Ecclesiae traditionem doctrinamque scrutatur, ex quibus nova semper cum veteribus congruentia profert.
First therefore the sacred Synod professes that God Himself has made known the way to the human race by which, serving Him, men may be able to be saved and blessed in Christ. This one true Religion we believe to subsist in the catholic and apostolic Church, to which the Lord Jesus entrusted the office of spreading it to all men, saying to the Apostles: "Go therefore and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you" (Mt 28:19-20). All men, however, are bound to seek the truth, especially in those things which regard God and His Church, and when known, to embrace and keep it.
¶365
Primum itaque profitetur Sacra Synodus Deum Ipsum viam generi humano notam fecisse per quam, Ipsi inserviendo, homines in Christo salvi et beati fieri possint. Hanc unicam veram Religionem subsistere credimus in catholica et apostolica Ecclesia, cui Dominus Iesus munus concredidit eam ad universos homines diffundendi, dicens Apostolis: Euntes ergo docete omnes gentes baptizantes eos in nomine Patris et Filii et Spiritus Sancti, docentes eos servare omnia quaecumque mandavi vobis (Mt 28,19-20). Homines vero cuncti tenentur veritatem, praesertim in iis quae Deum Eiusque Ecclesiam respiciunt, quaerere eamque cognitam amplecti ac servare.
Likewise, the sacred Synod professes that these duties touch and bind the conscience of men, and that truth imposes itself by no other way than by the force of truth itself, which gently and at the same time strongly penetrates minds. Moreover, since religious freedom, which men require in fulfilling the duty of worshipping God, respects immunity from coercion in civil society, it leaves intact the traditional catholic doctrine concerning the moral duty of men and societies toward the true religion and the one Church of Christ. Moreover, in treating of this religious freedom, the sacred Synod intends to develop the doctrine of recent Roman Pontiffs concerning the inviolable rights of the human person and concerning the juridical ordering of society.
¶366
Pariter vero profitetur Sacra Synodus officia haec hominum conscientiam tangere ac vincire, nec aliter veritatem sese imponere nisi vi ipsius veritatis, quae suaviter simul ac fortiter mentibus illabitur. Porro, quum libertas religiosa, quam homines in exsequendo officio Deum colendi exigunt, immunitatem a coercitione in societate civili respiciat, integram relinquit traditionalem doctrinam catholicam de morali hominum ac societatum officio erga veram religionem et unicam Christi Ecclesiam. Insuper, de hac libertate religiosa agens, Sacra Synodus recentiorum Summorum Pontificum doctrinam de inviolabilibus humanae personae iuribus necnon de iuridica ordinatione societatis evolvere intendit.
2 This Vatican Synod declares that the human person has a right to religious freedom. This freedom consists in this, that all men ought to be immune from coercion on the part of individuals or of social groups and of any human power, and in such a way that in religious matters neither is anyone forced to act against his conscience, nor prevented from acting according to his conscience, privately and publicly, either alone or associated with others, within due limits. Moreover, it declares that the right to religious freedom is truly founded on the very dignity of the human person, as it is known both by the revealed word of God and by reason itself. This right of the human person to religious freedom is to be acknowledged in the juridical ordering of society, that it may become a civil right.
¶367
I. GENERAL RATIONALE OF RELIGIOUS FREEDOM
I. LIBERTATIS RELIGIOSAE RATIO GENERALIS
Haec Vaticana Synodus declarat personam humanam ius habere ad libertatem religiosam. Huiusmodi libertas in eo consistit, quod omnes homines debent immunes esse a coercitione ex parte sive singulorum sive coetuum socialium et cuiusvis potestatis humanae, et ita quidem ut in re religiosa neque aliquis cogatur ad agendum contra suam conscientiam neque impediatur, quominus iuxta suam conscientiam agat privatim et publice, vel solus vel aliis consociatus, intra debitos limites. Insuper declarat ius ad libertatem religiosam esse revera fundatum in ipsa dignitate personae humanae, qualis et verbo Dei revelato et ipsa ratione cognoscitur. Hoc ius personae humanae ad libertatem religiosam in iuridica societatis ordinatione ita est agnoscendum, ut ius civile evadat.
According to their dignity, all men, because they are persons, namely endowed with reason and free will and therefore endowed with personal responsibility, are impelled by their own nature and are bound by a moral obligation to seek the truth, especially that which concerns religion. They are also bound to adhere to the truth when known and to order their whole life according to the demands of the truth. Men, however, cannot satisfy this obligation in a manner consonant with their own nature unless they enjoy psychological freedom at the same time as immunity from external coercion. The right to religious freedom is therefore founded not on the subjective disposition of the person but on his very nature. Therefore the right to this immunity persists even in those who do not satisfy the obligation of seeking the truth and adhering to it; nor can its exercise be impeded, provided the just public order is preserved.
¶368
Secundum dignitatem suam homines cuncti, quia personae sunt, ratione scilicet et libera voluntate praediti ideoque personali responsabilitate aucti, sua ipsorum natura impelluntur necnon morali tenentur obligatione ad veritatem quaerendam, illam imprimis quae religionem spectat. Tenentur quoque veritati cognitae adhaerere atque totam vitam suam iuxta exigentias veritatis ordinare. Huic autem obligationi satisfacere homines, modo suae propriae naturae consentaneo, non possunt nisi libertate psychologica simul atque immunitate a coercitione externa fruantur. Non ergo in subiectiva personae dispositione, sed in ipsa eius natura ius ad libertatem religiosam fundatur. Quamobrem ius ad hanc immunitatem perseverat etiam in iis qui obligationi quaerendi veritatem eique adhaerendi non satisfaciunt; eiusque exercitium impediri nequit dummodo iustus ordo publicus servetur.
3 Which becomes yet more clear to one considering that the supreme norm of human life is the divine law itself, eternal, objective, and universal, by which God, according to the counsel of His wisdom and love, orders, directs, and governs the whole world and the ways of the human community. Of this His law God makes man a partaker, so that man, by divine Providence gently disposing, may be able more and more to recognize the unchangeable truth. Wherefore each has the duty and therefore the right to seek the truth in religious matters, that by using suitable means he may prudently form right and true judgments of conscience.
¶369
Quae clarius adhuc patent consideranti supremam humanae vitae normam esse ipsam legem divinam, aeternam, obiectivam atque universalem, qua Deus consilio sapientiae et dilectionis suae mundum universum viasque communitatis humanae ordinat, dirigit, gubernat. Huius suae legis Deus hominem participem reddit, ita ut homo, providentia divina suaviter disponente, veritatem incommutabilem magis magisque agnoscere possit. Quapropter unusquisque officium ideoque et ius habet veritatem in re religiosa quaerendi ut sibi, mediis adhibitis idoneis, recta et vera conscientiae iudicia prudenter efformet.
Truth, however, is to be sought in a manner proper to the dignity of the human person and his social nature, namely by free investigation, with the help of teaching or instruction, communication, and dialogue, by which men set forth to one another the truth which they have found or think they have found, that they may help one another in seeking the truth; but once known, truth must be firmly adhered to by personal assent.
¶370
Veritas autem inquirenda est modo dignitati humanae personae eiusque naturae sociali proprio, libera scilicet inquisitione, ope magisterii seu institutionis, communicationis atque dialogi, quibus alii aliis exponunt veritatem quam invenerunt vel invenisse putant, ut sese invicem in veritate inquirenda adiuvent; veritati autem cognitae firmiter adhaerendum est assensu personali.
Man perceives and acknowledges the dictates of the divine law through his conscience, which he is bound faithfully to follow in all his activity, that he may come to God, his end. He is therefore not to be forced to act against his conscience. Nor is he to be prevented from acting according to his conscience, especially in matters religious. For the exercise of religion, by its very nature, consists chiefly in internal acts, voluntary and free, by which man directly orders himself to God: such acts can neither be commanded nor prohibited by mere human power. The very social nature of man, however, requires that he express internal acts of religion externally, communicate with others in religious matters, and profess his religion in a communal manner.
¶371
Dictamina vero legis divinae homo percipit et agnoscit mediante conscientia sua; quam tenetur fideliter sequi in universa sua activitate, ut ad Deum, finem suum, perveniat. Non est ergo cogendus, ut contra suam conscientiam agat. Sed neque impediendus est, quominus iuxta suam conscientiam operetur, praesertim in re religiosa. Exercitium namque religionis, ex ipsa eius indole, consistit imprimis in actibus internis voluntariis et liberis, quibus homo sese ad Deum directe ordinat: huiusmodi actus a potestate mere humana nec imperari nec prohiberi possunt. Ipsa autem socialis hominis natura exigit, ut homo internos religionis actus externe exprimat, cum aliis in re religiosa communicet, suam religionem modo communitario profiteatur.
Therefore it is an injury to the human person and to the very order established by God for men, if free exercise of religion in society is denied to man, the just public order being preserved. Moreover, religious acts, by which men privately and publicly order themselves to God from their mind's intention, by their nature transcend the earthly and temporal order of things. Therefore civil power, whose proper end is to take care of temporal common good, ought indeed to recognize and favor the religious life of citizens, but it is said to exceed its limits if it presumes to direct or impede religious acts.
¶372
Iniuria ergo humanae personae et ipsi ordini hominibus a Deo statuto fit, si homini denegetur liberum in societate religionis exercitium, iusto ordine publico servato. Praeterea actus religiosi, quibus homines privatim et publice sese ad Deum ex animi sententia ordinant, natura sua terrestrem et temporalem rerum ordinem transcendunt. Potestas igitur civilis, cuius finis proprius est bonum commune temporale curare, religiosam quidem civium vitam agnoscere eique favere debet, sed limites suos excedere dicenda est, si actus religiosos dirigere vel impedire praesumat.
4 Freedom, that is immunity from coercion in religious matters, which is due to individual persons, is also to be acknowledged to those acting in common. For religious communities are required by the social nature both of man and of religion itself. Therefore, to these communities, as long as the just exigencies of public order are not violated, immunity is of right due, that they may govern themselves according to their own norms, honor the Supreme Being with public worship, help their members in exercising religious life and sustain them with doctrine, and promote those institutions in which the members cooperate to order their own life according to their religious principles.
¶373
Libertas seu immunitas a coercitione in re religiosa, quae singulis personis competit, etiam ipsis in communi agentibus agnoscenda est. Communitates enim religiosae a sociali natura tum hominis tum ipsius religionis requiruntur. His igitur communitatibus, dummodo iustae exigentiae ordinis publici non violentur, iure debetur immunitas, ut secundum proprias normas sese regant, Numen supremum cultu publico honorent, membra sua in vita religiosa exercenda adiuvent et doctrina sustentent atque eas institutiones promoveant, in quibus membra cooperentur ad vitam propriam secundum sua principia religiosa ordinandam.
It likewise pertains to religious communities to have the right that they be not prevented by legal means or administrative action of civil power from selecting, educating, appointing, and transferring their own ministers, from communicating with religious authorities and communities living in other parts of the world, from erecting religious buildings, and from acquiring and enjoying suitable goods. Religious communities also have the right not to be prevented from publicly teaching and bearing witness to their faith by word and writing. In disseminating and introducing religious faith, however, one must always abstain from any kind of action that seems to savor of coercion or dishonest or less right persuasion, especially when dealing with the more simple or the needy. Such a manner of acting is to be considered as an abuse of one's own right and a violation of the right of others.
¶374
Communitatibus religiosis pariter competit ius, ne mediis legalibus vel actione administrativa potestatis civilis impediantur in suis propriis ministris seligendis, educandis, nominandis atque transferendis, in communicando cum auctoritatibus et communitatibus religiosis, quae in aliis orbis terrarum partibus degunt, in aedificiis religiosis erigendis, necnon in bonis congruis acquirendis et fruendis. Communitates religiosae ius etiam habent, ne impediantur in sua fide ore et scripto publice docenda atque testanda. In fide autem religiosa disseminanda et in usibus inducendis abstinendum semper est ab omni actionis genere, quod coercitionem vel suasionem inhonestam aut minus rectam sapere videatur, praesertim quando de rudioribus vel de egenis agitur. Talis modus agendi ut abusus iuris proprii et laesio iuris aliorum considerari debet.
Moreover, to religious freedom it pertains that religious communities be not prohibited from freely showing the singular power of their doctrine in ordering society and in vivifying all human activity. Finally, the right of men, moved by their own religious sense, freely to hold meetings or to form educational, cultural, charitable, social associations is founded on the social nature of man and on the very nature of religion.
¶375
Praeterea ad libertatem religiosam spectat, quod communitates religiosae non prohibeantur libere ostendere singularem suae doctrinae virtutem in ordinanda societate ac tota vivificanda activitate humana. Tandem in sociali hominis natura atque in ipsa indole religionis fundatur ius quo homines, suo ipsorum sensu religioso moti, libere possunt conventus habere vel associationes educativas, culturales, caritativas, sociales constituere.
5 To every family, as a society enjoying a proper and primordial right, it pertains to have the right freely to order its own domestic religious life under the direction of the parents. To parents, moreover, it pertains to determine the manner of religious education to be given to their children, according to their own religious persuasion. Therefore the right of parents to choose schools or other means of education in true freedom is to be recognized by civil power, nor are unjust burdens to be imposed on them directly or indirectly on account of this freedom of choice. Moreover, the rights of parents are violated if children are forced to attend school lessons which do not correspond to the religious persuasion of the parents, or if a single educational system is imposed from which all religious formation is wholly excluded.
¶376
Cuique familiae, utpote quae est societas proprio ac primordiali iure gaudens, competit ius ad libere ordinandam religiosam vitam suam domesticam sub moderatione parentum. His autem competit ius ad determinandam rationem institutionis religiosae suis liberis tradendae, iuxta suam propriam religiosam persuasionem. Itaque a civili potestate agnoscendum est ius parentum deligendi, vera cum libertate, scholas vel alia educationis media, neque ob hanc electionis libertatem sunt eis iniusta onera sive directe sive indirecte imponenda. Praeterea iura parentum violantur, si liberi ad frequentandas lectiones scholares cogantur quae parentum persuasioni religiosae non correspondeant, vel si unica imponatur educationis ratio, ex qua formatio religiosa omnino excludatur.
6 Since the common good of society, which is the sum of those conditions of social life by which men can more fully and conveniently attain their own perfection, especially consists in the preserved rights and duties of the human person, the care of the right to religious freedom pertains both to citizens and to social groups and to civil powers and to the Church and to other religious communities, each in its own manner, according to their duty toward the common good. To protect and promote the inviolable rights of man essentially pertains to the office of every civil power. Therefore civil power ought to undertake the protection of the religious freedom of all citizens by just laws and by other suitable means effectively, and to supply favorable conditions for fostering religious life, that citizens may truly be able to exercise the rights of religion and to fulfill its duties, and society itself may enjoy the goods of justice and peace, which proceed from the fidelity of men toward God and His holy will.
¶377
Cum societatis commune bonum, quod est summa earum vitae socialis condicionum, quibus homines suam ipsorum perfectionem possunt plenius atque expeditius consequi, maxime in humanae personae servatis iuribus et officiis consistat, cura iuris ad libertatem religiosam tum ad cives tum ad coetus sociales tum ad potestates civiles tum ad Ecclesiam aliasque communitates religiosas spectat, modo unicuique proprio, pro eorum erga bonum commune officio. Inviolabilia hominis iura tueri ac promovere ad cuiusvis potestatis civilis officium essentialiter pertinet. Debet igitur potestas civilis per iustas leges et per alia media apta efficaciter suscipere tutelam libertatis religiosae omnium civium, ac propitias suppeditare condiciones ad vitam religiosam fovendam, ut cives revera religionis iura exercere eiusdemque officia adimplere valeant et ipsa societas fruatur bonis iustitiae et pacis, quae proveniunt ex fidelitate hominum erga Deum Eiusque sanctam voluntatem.
If, the particular circumstances of peoples being attended to, a special civil recognition is given to one religious community in the juridical ordering of the city, it is necessary that at the same time for all citizens and religious communities the right to freedom in religious matters be acknowledged and observed. Finally, by civil power it is to be provided that the juridical equality of citizens, which itself pertains to the common good of society, never be openly or secretly injured on account of religious reasons, nor that discrimination be made among them. Hence it follows that it is unlawful for public power, by force or fear or other means, to impose on citizens the profession or rejection of any religion, or to prevent anyone from entering or leaving a religious community. So much the more is it acting against the will of God and the sacred rights of the person and the family of nations when violence is used in any way to destroy or repress religion either in the whole human race or in any region or in a certain group.
¶378
Si attentis populorum circumstantiis peculiaribus uni communitati religiosae specialis civilis agnitio in iuridica civitatis ordinatione tribuitur, necesse est ut simul omnibus civibus et communitatibus religiosis ius ad libertatem in re religiosa agnoscatur et observetur. Denique a potestate civili providendum est, ne civium aequalitas iuridica, quae ipsa ad commune societatis bonum pertinet, unquam sive aperte sive occulte laedatur propter rationes religiosas, neve inter eos discriminatio fiat. Hinc sequitur nefas esse potestati publicae, per vim vel metum aut alia media civibus imponere professionem aut reiectionem cuiusvis religionis, vel impedire quominus quisquam communitatem religiosam aut ingrediatur aut relinquat. Eo magis contra voluntatem Dei et contra sacra personae et familiae gentium iura agitur, quando vis quocumque modo adhibeatur ad religionem delendam vel cohibendam sive in toto genere humano sive in aliqua regione sive in determinato coetu.
7 The right to freedom in religious matters is exercised in human society, therefore its use is subject to certain moderating norms. In the use of all freedoms the moral principle of personal and social responsibility is to be observed: in exercising their rights, individual men and social groups are bound by moral law to have regard for the rights of others and their own duties toward others and the common good of all. All must act toward all according to justice and humanity. Moreover, since civil society has the right to protect itself against abuses which may be had under pretext of religious freedom, it chiefly pertains to civil power to provide such protection; which, however, must be done not arbitrarily or by iniquely favoring one party, but according to juridical norms, conformable to the objective moral order, which are demanded by the effective protection of the rights of all citizens and their peaceful composition, and by the sufficient care of that honest public peace which is ordered conviviality in true justice, and by the due custody of public morality. All these constitute a fundamental part of the common good and come under the notion of public order. Moreover, the custom of integral freedom in society is to be preserved, according to which freedom ought to be acknowledged to man as much as possible, and not restricted unless when and insofar as necessary.
¶379
Ius ad libertatem in re religiosa exercetur in societate humana, ideoque eius usus quibusdam normis moderantibus obnoxius est. In usu omnium libertatum observandum est principium morale responsabilitatis personalis et socialis: in iuribus suis exercendis singuli homines coetusque sociales lege morali obligantur rationem habere et iurium aliorum et suorum erga alios officiorum et boni omnium communis. Cum omnibus secundum iustitiam et humanitatem agendum est. Praeterea cum societas civilis ius habet sese protegendi contra abusus qui haberi possint sub praetextu libertatis religiosae, praecipue ad potestatem civilem pertinet huiusmodi protectionem praestare; quod tamen fieri debet non modo arbitrario aut uni parti inique favendo, sed secundum normas iuridicas, ordini morali obiectivo conformes, quae postulantur ab efficaci iurium tutela pro omnibus civibus eorumque pacifica compositione, et a sufficienti cura istius honestae pacis publicae quae est ordinata conviventia in vera iustitia, et a debita custodia publicae moralitatis. Haec omnia partem boni communis fundamentalem constituunt et sub ratione ordinis publici veniunt. Ceterum servanda est integrae libertatis consuetudo in societate, secundum quam libertas debet quam maxime homini agnosci, nec restringenda est nisi quando et prout est necessarium.
8 Men of our age are pressed in various ways and come into peril lest they be deprived of their own free counsel. On the other hand, not a few seem so inclined that under the appearance of freedom they reject all subjection and make light of due obedience. Wherefore this Vatican Synod exhorts all, especially those who have care of educating others, that they strive to form men who, obedient to the moral order, obey legitimate authority and are lovers of genuine freedom; men namely who, judging matters in the light of truth by their own counsel, order their activities with a sense of responsibility, and strive to pursue whatever things are true and just, willingly associating their work with others. Religious freedom, therefore, ought also to serve and to be ordered to this, that men may act with greater responsibility in fulfilling their own duties in social life.
¶380
Nostrae aetatis homines varia ratione premuntur et in periculum veniunt ne proprio libero consilio destituantur. Ex altera autem parte non pauci ita propensi videntur, ut specie libertatis omnem subiectionem reiciant ac debitam oboedientiam parvi faciant. Quapropter haec Vaticana Synodus omnes hortatur, praesertim vero eos qui curam habent alios educandi, ut homines formare satagant, qui ordini morali obsequentes legitimae auctoritati oboediant et genuinae libertatis amatores sint; homines nempe, qui proprio consilio res in luce veritatis diiudicent, activitates suas cum sensu responsabilitatis disponant, et quaecumque sunt vera atque iusta prosequi nitantur, operam suam libenter cum ceteris consociando. Religiosa igitur libertas etiam ad hoc inservire et ordinari debet, ut homines in suis ipsorum officiis adimplendis in vita sociali maiore cum responsabilitate agant.
9 What this Vatican Synod declares concerning the right of man to religious freedom has its foundation in the dignity of the person, the exigencies of which have become more fully known to human reason through the experience of centuries. Indeed, this doctrine of freedom has its roots in divine Revelation, wherefore it is to be the more sacredly kept by Christians. For although Revelation does not expressly affirm the right to immunity from external coercion in religious matters, it nevertheless lays open the dignity of the human person in all its amplitude, demonstrates the respect of Christ for man's freedom in fulfilling the duty of believing the word of God, and teaches us concerning the spirit which disciples of such a Master ought to acknowledge and follow in all things. By which all, the general principles are illustrated upon which the doctrine of this Declaration concerning religious freedom is founded. Especially, religious freedom in society is fully congruent with the freedom of the act of Christian faith.

II. LIBERTAS RELIGIOSA SUB LUCE REVELATIONIS
II. RELIGIOUS FREEDOM IN THE LIGHT OF REVELATION
Quae de iure hominis ad libertatem religiosam declarat haec Vaticana Synodus, fundamentum habent in dignitate personae, cuius exigentiae rationi humanae plenius innotuerunt per saeculorum experientiam. Immo haec doctrina de libertate radices habet in divina Revelatione, quapropter eo magis a Christianis sancte servanda est. Quamvis enim Revelatio non expresse affirmet ius ad immunitatem ab externa coercitione in re religiosa, tamen humanae personae dignitatem in tota eius amplitudine patefacit, observantiam Christi erga hominis libertatem in exsequendo officio credendi verbo Dei demonstrat, atque de spiritu nos edocet, quem discipuli talis Magistri debent in omnibus agnoscere et sequi. Quibus omnibus principia generalia illustrantur super quae fundatur doctrina huius Declarationis de libertate religiosa. Praesertim libertas religiosa in societate plene est cum libertate actus fidei christianae congrua.
10 It is capital among the principal doctrines of catholic doctrine, contained in the word of God and constantly preached by the Fathers, that man ought voluntarily to respond to God by believing; therefore no one is to be forced to embrace the faith against his will. For the act of faith is by its very nature voluntary, since man, redeemed by Christ the Savior and called to the adoption of sons through Jesus Christ, cannot adhere to God revealing Himself unless, the Father drawing him (cf. Jn 6:44), he renders to God the rational and free submission of faith. Therefore it is fully consonant with the nature of faith that in religious matters all coercion on the part of men be excluded. And accordingly the reason of religious freedom contributes not a little to foster that state of affairs in which men can be readily invited to the Christian faith, spontaneously embrace it, and actively confess it in their whole manner of life.
¶382
Caput est ex praecipuis doctrinae catholicae, in verbo Dei contentum et a Patribus constanter praedicatum, hominem debere Deo voluntarie respondere credendo; invitum proinde neminem esse cogendum ad amplectendam fidem. Etenim actus fidei ipsa sua natura voluntarius est, cum homo, a Christo Salvatore redemptus et in adoptionem filiorum per Iesum Christum vocatus, Deo Sese revelanti adhaerere non possit, nisi Patre eum trahente (cf. Jn 6,44), rationabile liberumque Deo praestiterit fidei obsequium. Indoli ergo fidei plene consonum est ut, in re religiosa, quodvis genus coercitionis ex parte hominum excludatur. Ac proinde ratio libertatis religiosae haud parum confert ad illum rerum statum fovendum, in quo homines expedite possint invitari ad fidem christianam, illam sponte amplecti atque eam in tota vitae ratione actuose confiteri.
11 God indeed calls men to serve Him in spirit and truth, hence they are bound in conscience, not coerced. For He has regard for the dignity of the human person created by Himself, who ought to be led by his own counsel and enjoy freedom. This appeared most of all in Christ Jesus, in whom God perfectly manifested Himself and His ways. For Christ, who is our Master and Lord, and the same gentle and humble of heart, attracted and invited disciples patiently. He indeed supported and confirmed His preaching by miracles, to excite and prove the faith of hearers, not to exercise coercion on them. He certainly exprobrated the unbelief of hearers, but left vengeance to God on the day of judgment. Sending the Apostles into the world, He said to them: "He who believes and is baptized shall be saved; he who does not believe shall be condemned" (Mk 16:16). He himself, however, knowing that zizania is sown with the wheat, commanded that both be allowed to grow until the harvest which will be at the consummation of the world. Unwilling to be a political Messiah ruling by force, He preferred to call Himself the Son of Man, who came to minister and to give His life as a redemption for many (Mk 10:45). He showed Himself as the perfect Servant of God, who "will not break the bruised reed, nor extinguish the smoking flax" (Mt 12:20). He acknowledged civil power and its rights, commanding tribute to be given to Caesar, but clearly warned that the superior rights of God are to be kept: "Render therefore to Caesar the things that are Caesar's, and to God the things that are God's" (Mt 22:21). Finally, completing the work of redemption on the cross, by which He acquired salvation and true freedom for men, He perfected His revelation. For He bore witness to the truth, but was unwilling to impose it by force on contradictors. For His kingdom is not vindicated by striking, but is established by testifying and hearing the truth, and grows by the love with which Christ lifted up on the cross draws all to Himself.
¶383
Deus quidem homines ad inserviendum Sibi in spiritu et veritate vocat, unde ipsi in conscientia vinciuntur, non vero coercentur. Rationem enim habet dignitatis personae humanae ab Ipso conditae, quae proprio consilio duci et libertate frui debet. Hoc autem summe apparuit in Christo Iesu, in quo Deus Seipsum ac vias suas perfecte manifestavit. Etenim Christus, qui Magister et Dominus est noster, idemque mitis et humilis corde, discipulos patienter allexit et invitavit. Miraculis utique praedicationem suam suffulsit et confirmavit, ut fidem auditorum excitaret atque comprobaret, non ut in eos coercitionem exerceret. Incredulitatem audientium certe exprobravit, sed vindictam Deo in diem Iudicii relinquendo. Mittens Apostolos in mundum dixit eis: Qui crediderit et baptizatus fuerit salvus erit; qui vero non crediderit condemnabitur (Mc 16,16). Ipse vero, agnoscens zizaniam cum tritico seminatam, iussit sinere utraque crescere usque ad messem quae fiet in consummatione saeculi. Nolens esse Messias politicus et vi dominans, maluit se dicere Filium Hominis qui venit ut ministraret et daret animam suam redemptionem pro multis (Mc 10,45). Sese praebuit ut perfectum Servum Dei, qui harundinem quassatam non confringet et linum fumigans non extinguet (Mt 12,20). Potestatem civilem eiusque iura agnovit, iubens censum dari Caesari, clare autem monuit servanda esse iura superiora Dei: Reddite ergo quae sunt Caesaris Caesari, et quae sunt Dei Deo (Mt 22,21). Tandem in opere redemptionis in cruce complendo, quo salutem et veram libertatem hominibus acquireret, revelationem suam perfecit. Testimonium enim perhibuit veritati, eam tamen contradicentibus vi imponere noluit. Regnum enim eius non percutiendo vindicatur, sed stabilitur testificando et audiendo veritatem, crescit autem amore, quo Christus exaltatus in cruce homines ad Seipsum trahit.
The Apostles, taught by the word and example of Christ, followed the same way. From the very beginnings of the Church, the disciples of Christ labored to convert men to confessing Christ the Lord, not by coercive action nor by artifices unworthy of the Gospel, but especially by the power of the word of God. They strongly announced to all the purpose of God the Savior, who wills all men to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth (1 Tim 2:4); at the same time, however, they respected the weak even if they were in error, thus showing how each of us will give an account for himself to God (Rom 14:12) and is bound to obey his conscience. Like Christ, the Apostles were always intent on bearing witness to the truth of God, speaking the word of God with confidence (Acts 4:31) more abundantly before the people and princes. For they firmly held that the Gospel itself is indeed the power of God for salvation to every believer. Therefore, all carnal arms having been spurned, following the example of meekness and modesty of Christ, they preached the word of God, fully trusting in the divine power of this word to destroy the powers opposing God and to reduce men to the faith and obedience of Christ. Like the Master, so the Apostles acknowledged legitimate civil authority: "For there is no power but from God," teaches the Apostle, whence he commands: "Let every soul be subject to higher powers... he who resists the power, resists the ordinance of God" (Rom 13:1-2). At the same time, however, they did not fear to contradict public power opposing the holy will of God: "We ought to obey God rather than men" (Acts 5:29). Innumerable martyrs and faithful have followed this way through the ages and the world.
¶384
Apostoli, Christi verbo et exemplo edocti, eamdem viam secuti sunt. Ab ipsis Ecclesiae exordiis discipuli Christi adlaborarunt, ut homines ad Christum Dominum confitendum converterent, non actione coercitiva neque artificiis Evangelio indignis, sed imprimis virtute verbi Dei. Fortiter omnibus nuntiabant propositum Salvatoris Dei, qui omnes homines vult salvos fieri et ad agnitionem veritatis venire (1Tm 2,4); simul autem verebantur debiles etiamsi in errore versabantur, sic ostendentes quomodo unusquisque nostrum pro se rationem reddet Deo (Rm 14,12) et in tantum teneatur conscientiae suae oboedire. Sicuti Christus, Apostoli intenti semper fuerunt ad testimonium reddendum veritati Dei, abundantius audentes coram populo et principibus loqui verbum Dei cum fiducia (Ac 4,31). Firma enim fide tenebant ipsum Evangelium revera esse virtutem Dei in salutem omni credenti. Omnibus ergo spretis armis carnalibus, exemplum mansuetudinis et modestiae Christi sequentes, verbum Dei praedicaverunt plene confisi divina huius verbi virtute ad potestates Deo adversas destruendas atque homines ad fidem et obsequium Christi reducendos. Sicut Magister ita et Apostoli auctoritatem legitimam civilem agnoverunt: Non est enim potestas nisi a Deo docet Apostolus, qui exinde iubet: Omnis anima potestatibus sublimioribus subdita sit; ... qui resistit potestati, Dei ordinationi resistit (Rm 13,1-2). Simul autem non timuerunt contradicere potestati publicae se sanctae Dei voluntati opponenti: Oboedire oportet Deo magis quam hominibus (Ac 5,29). Hanc viam secuti sunt innumeri martyres et fideles per saecula et per orbem.
12 The Church, therefore, faithful to the gospel truth, follows the way of Christ and the Apostles when she acknowledges and fosters the reason of religious freedom as consonant with the dignity of man and the revelation of God. She has kept and handed down the doctrine received from the Master and the Apostles, through the course of times. Although in the life of the People of God, pilgrimaging through the vicissitudes of human history, a manner of acting less conformable to the gospel spirit, indeed contrary, has sometimes existed, yet the doctrine of the Church that no one is to be forced to the faith has always remained. The evangelical leaven has so long operated in the minds of men and contributed much, that in the course of time men have more widely recognized the dignity of the human person, and the persuasion has matured that in religious matters immunity from any human coercion is to be kept in society.
¶385
Ecclesia igitur, evangelicae veritati fidelis, viam Christi et Apostolorum sequitur quando rationem libertatis religiosae tamquam dignitati hominis et Dei revelationi consonam agnoscit eamque fovet. Doctrinam a Magistro et ab Apostolis acceptam, decursu temporum, custodivit et tradidit. Etsi in vita Populi Dei, per vicissitudines historiae humanae peregrinantis, interdum exstitit modus agendi spiritui evangelico minus conformis, immo contrarius, semper tamen mansit Ecclesiae doctrina neminem esse ad fidem cogendum. Evangelicum fermentum in mentibus hominum sic diu est operatum atque multum contulit, ut homines temporum decursu latius agnoscerent dignitatem personae suae et maturesceret persuasio in re religiosa ipsam immunem servandam esse in civitate a quacumque humana coercitione.
13 Among those things which pertain to the good of the Church, indeed to the good of the earthly city itself, and are always and everywhere to be kept and defended from all injury, this certainly is most excellent, that the Church enjoy so great freedom of action as the care of men's salvation requires. For this freedom is sacred, with which the Only-begotten Son of God enriched the Church acquired by His blood. Indeed it is so proper to the Church that those who impugn it act against the will of God. The freedom of the Church is a fundamental principle in the relations between the Church and public powers and the whole civil order. In human society and before any public power, the Church claims freedom for herself as a spiritual authority, constituted by Christ the Lord, to whom by divine mandate the office of going into the whole world and preaching the Gospel to every creature pertains. Likewise the Church claims freedom for herself as also a society of men who have the right to live in civil society according to the precepts of the Christian faith.
¶386
Inter ea quae ad bonum Ecclesiae, immo ad bonum ipsius terrenae civitatis spectant et ubique semperque servanda sunt atque ab omni iniuria defendenda, illud certe praestantissimum est, ut Ecclesia tanta perfruatur agendi libertate, quantam salus hominum curanda requirat. Haec enim libertas sacra est, qua Unigenitus Dei Filius ditavit Ecclesiam acquisitam sanguine suo. Ecclesiae sane adeo propria est, ut qui eam impugnant, iidem contra Dei voluntatem agant. Libertas Ecclesiae est principium fundamentale in relationibus inter Ecclesiam et potestates publicas totumque ordinem civilem. In societate humana et coram quavis potestate publica Ecclesia sibi vindicat libertatem, utpote auctoritas spiritualis, a Christo Domino constituta, cui ex divino mandato incumbit officium eundi in mundum universum et Evangelium praedicandi omni creaturae. Libertatem pariter sibi vindicat Ecclesia prout est etiam societas hominum qui iure gaudent vivendi in societate civili secundum fidei christianae praescripta.
Now, if the reason of religious freedom flourishes, not only proclaimed by words nor only sanctioned by laws, but also reduced to practice with sincerity, then at length the Church obtains a stable condition both in law and in fact for the necessary independence to carry out the divine mission, which ecclesiastical authorities have more and more insistently claimed in society. At the same time, Christ's faithful, like other men, enjoy civil right not to be prevented in living according to conscience. Therefore concord flourishes between the freedom of the Church and that religious freedom which is to be acknowledged as a right to all men and communities and sanctioned in juridical ordering.
¶387
Iamvero, si viget ratio libertatis religiosae non solum verbis proclamata neque solum legibus sancita, sed etiam cum sinceritate in praxim deducta, tunc demum Ecclesia stabilem obtinet et iuris et facti condicionem ad necessariam in missione divina exsequenda independentiam, quam auctoritates ecclesiasticae in societate presse pressiusque vindicarunt. Simulque Christifideles, sicut et ceteri homines, iure civili gaudent ne impediantur in vita sua iuxta conscientiam agenda. Concordia igitur viget inter libertatem Ecclesiae et libertatem illam religiosam, quae omnibus hominibus et communitatibus est tanquam ius agnoscenda et in ordinatione iuridica sancienda.
14 The Catholic Church, that she may obey the divine mandate: "Teach all nations" (Mt 28:19), ought to labor with great care that the word of God may run and be glorified (2 Th 3:1). Therefore the Church earnestly asks that, first of all by her sons, "supplications, prayers, intercessions, thanksgivings be made for all men... For this is good and acceptable before God our Savior, who wills all men to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth" (1 Tim 2:1-4). Christ's faithful, however, in forming their own conscience, ought diligently to attend to the sacred and certain doctrine of the Church. For by the will of Christ, the Catholic Church is the teacher of truth, and it is her office to pronounce and authentically teach the Truth which is Christ, and at the same time by her authority to declare and confirm principles of the moral order flowing from human nature itself. Moreover, Christians, walking in wisdom toward those who are outside, in the Holy Spirit, in unaffected charity, in the word of truth (2 Cor 6:6-7), should strive to spread the light of life with all confidence and apostolic fortitude, even to the shedding of blood. For the disciple is gravely bound to the Master Christ with the duty of more and more fully knowing the truth received from Him, faithfully announcing it, and strenuously defending it, excluding means contrary to the gospel spirit. At the same time, however, the charity of Christ urges him to act lovingly, prudently, patiently with men who are in error or ignorance concerning the faith. Regard is therefore to be had both to the duties toward Christ the life-giving Word which is to be preached, and to the rights of the human person, and to the measure of grace given by God through Christ to man, who is invited to faith to be accepted and professed freely.
¶388
Ecclesia catholica, ut divino obtemperet mandato: docete omnes gentes (Mt 28,19), impensa cura adlaborare debet ut sermo Dei currat et clarificetur (2Th 3,1). Enixe igitur rogat Ecclesia, ut a filiis suis primum omnium fiant obsecrationes, orationes, postulationes, gratiarum actiones pro omnibus hominibus... Hoc enim bonum est et acceptum coram Salvatore nostro Deo, qui omnes homines vult salvos fieri et ad agnitionem veritatis venire (1Tm 2,1-4). Christifideles autem in sua efformanda conscientia diligenter attendere debent ad sacram certamque Ecclesiae doctrinam. Christi enim voluntate Ecclesia catholica magistra est veritatis, eiusque munus est, ut Veritatem quae Christus est enuntiet atque authentice doceat, simulque principia ordinis moralis, ex ipsa natura humana profluentia, auctoritate sua declaret atque confirmet. Insuper Christiani, in sapientia ambulantes ad eos qui foris sunt, in Spiritu Sancto, in caritate non ficta, in verbo veritatis (2Co 6,6-7), lumen vitae cum omni fiducia et fortitudine apostolica, ad sanguinis usque effusionem, diffundere satagant. Etenim discipulus erga Christum Magistrum gravi adstringitur officio, veritatem ab Eo receptam plenius in dies cognoscendi, annuntiandi fideliter, strenueque defendendi, exclusis mediis spiritui evangelico contrariis. Simul tamen caritas Christi urget eum, ut amanter prudenter patienter agat cum hominibus, qui in errore vel ignorantia circa fidem versantur. Respiciendum igitur est tum ad officia erga Christum Verbum vivificans quod praedicandum est, tum ad humanae personae iura, tum ad mensuram gratiae a Deo per Christum tributam homini, qui ad fidem sponte accipiendam et profitendam invitatur.
15 It is clear therefore that the men of the present age desire that they may freely profess their religion privately and publicly, indeed religious freedom is already declared as a civil right in most Constitutions and solemnly acknowledged in international documents. Yet there are not lacking regimes in which, although freedom of religious worship is acknowledged in their Constitution, yet the public powers themselves strive to turn citizens away from professing religion and to make life very difficult and perilous for religious communities. This holy Synod, joyfully saluting those favorable signs of this time, but denouncing these deplorable facts with grief, exhorts Catholics, and also entreats all men, that they most attentively consider how necessary religious freedom is in the present condition especially of the human family. For it is manifest that all nations are more and more becoming one, men of diverse culture and religion are being bound together by closer relations, and the consciousness of each one's own responsibility increases. Therefore, that peaceful relations and concord may be established and strengthened in the human race, it is required that religious freedom be everywhere protected by effective juridical protection, and that the supreme duties and rights of men to lead religious life freely in society be observed.
¶389
Constat igitur praesentis aetatis homines optare ut libere possint religionem privatim publiceque profiteri, immo libertatem religiosam in plerisque Constitutionibus iam ut ius civile declarari et documentis internationalibus sollemniter agnosci. At non desunt regimina in quibus, etsi in eorum Constitutione libertas cultus religiosi agnoscitur, tamen ipsae publicae potestates conantur cives a religione profitenda removere et communitatibus religiosis vitam perdifficilem ac periclitantem reddere. Illa fausta huius temporis signa laeto animo salutans, haec vero deploranda facta cum maerore denuntians, Sacra Synodus Catholicos hortatur, exorat autem homines universos, ut perattente considerent quantopere libertas religiosa necessaria sit in praesenti potissimum familiae humanae condicione. Manifestum est enim cunctas gentes magis in dies unum fieri, homines diversae culturae et religionis arctioribus inter se devinciri rationibus, augeri denique conscientiam propriae cuiusque responsabilitatis. Proinde ut pacificae relationes et concordia in genere humano instaurentur et firmentur, requiritur ut ubique terrarum libertas religiosa efficaci tutela iuridica muniatur atque observentur suprema hominum officia et iura ad vitam religiosam libere in societate ducendam.
May God and Father of all bring it about that the human family, carefully keeping the reason of religious freedom in society, through the grace of Christ and the power of the Holy Spirit, be led to that sublime and perpetual freedom of the glory of the sons of God (Rom 8:21).
¶390
Faxit Deus et Pater omnium ut familia humana, diligenter servata libertatis religiosae ratione in societate, per gratiam Christi et virtutem Spiritus Sancti adducatur ad sublimem illam ac perennem libertatem gloriae filiorum Dei (Rm 8,21).
1 The joy and hope, the grief and anguish of the men of this time, especially of the poor and of all those who are afflicted, are the joy and hope, the grief and anguish of the disciples of Christ as well, and nothing truly human is found that does not resound in their hearts. For their community is made up of men, who, united in Christ, are led by the Holy Spirit in their pilgrimage to the Kingdom of the Father and have received the message of salvation to be proposed to all. Therefore they themselves truly experience themselves intimately joined with the human race and its history.
¶391
GAUDIUM ET SPES
CONSTITUTIO PASTORALIS DE ECCLESIA IN MUNDO HUIUS TEMPORIS
PASTORAL CONSTITUTION ON THE CHURCH IN THE MODERN WORLD
De intima coniunctione Ecclesiae cum tota familia gentium
ON THE INTIMATE CONNECTION OF THE CHURCH WITH THE WHOLE FAMILY OF NATIONS
GAUDIUM ET SPES, luctus et angor hominum huius temporis, pauperum praesertim et quorumvis amictorum, gaudium sunt et spes, luctus et angor etiam Christi discipulorum nihilque vere humanum invenitur, quod in corde eorum non resonet. Ipsorum enim communitas ex hominibus coalescit, qui, in Christo coadunati, a Spiritu Sancto diriguntur in sua ad Regnum Patris peregrinatione et nuntium salutis omnibus proponendum acceperunt. Quapropter ipsa cum genere humano eiusque historia se revera intime coniunctam experitur.
2 Therefore the Second Vatican Council, having more deeply investigated the mystery of the Church, now unhesitatingly turns its discourse not only to the sons of the Church and to all who invoke the name of Christ, but to all men, wishing to set forth to all how the Church conceives its presence and activity in the modern world. It therefore has before its eyes the world of men, namely the whole human family with the totality of things among which it lives; the world, the theater of the history of the human race, marked by its industry, defeats, and victories; the world, which Christ's faithful believe to be created and conserved by the love of the Creator, placed indeed under the slavery of sin, but freed by Christ crucified and risen, the power of the Evil One broken, that according to the purpose of God it may be transformed and attain to its consummation.
¶392
Ad quosnam Concilium sermonem dirigat
To whom the Council directs its discourse
Ideo Concilium Vaticanum Secundum, mysterio Ecclesiae penitius investigato, iam non ad solos Ecclesiae filios omnesque Christi nomen invocantes, sed ad universos homines incunctanter sermonem convertit, omnibus exponere cupiens quomodo Ecclesiae praesentiam ac navitatem in mundo hodierno concipiat. Mundum igitur hominum prae oculis habet seu universam familiam humanam cum universitate rerum inter quas vivit; mundum, teatrum historiae generis humani, eiusque industria, cladibus ac victoriis signatum; mundum, quem christifideles credunt ex amore Creatoris conditum et conservatum, sub peccati quidem servitute positum, sed a Christo crucifixo et resurgente, fracta potestate Maligni, liberatum, ut secundum propositum Dei transformetur et ad consummationem perveniat.
3 In our days, however, the human race, astonished by its own discoveries and its own power, yet often anxiously agitates questions concerning the present development of the world, concerning the place and office of man in the universe, concerning the meaning of its individual and collective effort, finally concerning the ultimate end of things and of men. Therefore the Council, testifying and setting forth the faith of the whole people of God, gathered by Christ, cannot demonstrate its connection, respect, and love toward the whole human family in which it is inserted more eloquently than by entering into dialogue with it concerning those various problems, bringing light from the Gospel, and supplying to the human race salutary forces, which the Church itself, led by the Holy Spirit, receives from its Founder. For the human person is to be saved, and human society is to be restored. Man, therefore, and indeed one and whole, with body and soul, heart and conscience, mind and will, will be the hinge of our whole explanation. Therefore the sacred Synod, professing the highest vocation of man and asserting that a divine seed is inserted in him, offers to the human race the sincere cooperation of the Church for instituting that fraternity of all which corresponds to this vocation. The Church is moved by no earthly ambition, but intends only one thing: namely, under the guidance of the Paraclete Spirit, to continue the work of Christ Himself, who came into the world to bear witness to the truth, to save, not to judge, to minister, not to be ministered unto.
¶393
De ministerio homini praebendo
On the ministry to be rendered to man
Nostris autem diebus, genus humanum, de propriis inventis propriaque potentia admiratione commotum, saepe tamen anxias agitat quaestiones de hodierna mundi evolutione, de loco et munere hominis in orbe universo, de sui individualis et collectivi conaminis sensu, denique de ultimo rerum hominumque fine. Quapropter Concilium, fidem universi populi Dei, a Christo congregati, testificans et exponens, ipsius coniunctionem, observantiam ac dilectionem erga totam hominum familiam cui inseritur, eloquentius demonstrare non valet quam instituendo cum ea de variis illis problematibus colloquium, lumen afferendo ex Evangelio depromptum, atque humano generi salutares vires suppeditando, quae ipsa Ecclesia, Spiritu Sancto ducente, a Fundatore suo accipit. Hominis enim persona salvanda est humanaque societas instauranda. Homo igitur, et quidem unus ac totus, cum corpore et anima, corde et conscientia, mente et voluntate, totius nostrae explanationis cardo erit. Ideo Sacra Synodus, altissimam vocationem hominis profitens et divinum quoddam semen in eo insertum asseverans, generi humano sinceram cooperationem Ecclesiae offert ad instituendam eam omnium fraternitatem quae huic vocationi respondeat. Nulla ambitione terrestri movetur Ecclesia, sed unum tantum intendit: nempe, Spiritus Paracliti ductu, opus ipsius continuare Christi, qui in mundum venit ut testimonium perhiberet veritati, ut salvaret, non ut iudicaret, ut ministraret, non ut sibi ministraretur.
4 To carry out such an office, it is the duty of the Church at all times to scrutinize the signs of the times and to interpret them under the light of the Gospel; that, in a manner accommodated to each generation, it may be able to respond to the perennial questions of men concerning the meaning of present and future life and concerning their mutual relation. It is necessary therefore that the world in which we live and its expectations, aspirations, and often dramatic character be known and understood. Some of the more principal characteristics of the modern world can be delineated as follows. Today the human race is in a new age of its history in which profound and rapid changes gradually extend to the whole world. Excited by the intelligence and creative industry of man, they recoil upon man himself, upon his judgments and individual and collective desires, upon his manner of thinking and acting both concerning things and concerning men. Thus we can already speak of a true social and cultural transformation, which also redounds upon religious life. As happens in any crisis of growth, this transformation does not carry slight difficulties. Thus while man extends his power so widely, he is not always able to reduce it to his service. While he strives more deeply to penetrate the more intimate recesses of his own mind, he often appears more uncertain about himself. While he gradually more clearly discovers the laws of social life, he hesitates about the direction to be impressed upon it. Never has the human race abounded in so many riches, faculties, and economic power, and yet a huge part of the inhabitants of the world is tormented by famine and poverty, and countless numbers labor wholly in illiteracy. Never have men had so acute a sense of freedom as today, while at the same time new kinds of social and psychical servitude arise. While the world so vividly feels its unity and the dependence of individuals upon one another in necessary solidarity, it is nevertheless gravely torn into opposite parts by struggling forces; for acute political, social, economic, racial, and ideological dissensions still persist, and the danger of war destroying all things even to the depths is not lacking. While the communication of ideas increases, the very words by which important concepts are expressed take on rather different senses in distinct ideologies. Finally, a more perfect temporal ordering is sedulously sought, while spiritual growth does not progress equally. Affected by so many complex conditions, many of our contemporaries are prevented from ever duly recognizing perennial values and at the same time from rightly composing them with newly discovered things; hence, driven between hope and anguish, questioning the present course of events, they are pressed by anxiety. This course of events provokes and even constrains men to respond.
¶394
Expositio introductiva: DE HOMINIS CONDICIONE IN MUNDO HODIERNO
EXPOSITIO INTRODUCTIVA: ON THE CONDITION OF MAN IN THE MODERN WORLD
De spe et angore
On hope and anguish
Ad tale munus exsequendum, per omne tempus Ecclesiae officium incumbit signa temporum perscrutandi et sub Evangelii luce interpretandi; ita ut, modo unicuique generationi accommodato, ad perennes hominum interrogationes de sensu vitae praesentis et futurae deque earum mutua relatione respondere possit. Oportet itaque ut mundus in quo vivimus necnon eius expectationes, appetitiones et indoles saepe dramatica cognoscantur et intelligantur. Quaedam autem principaliores mundi hodierni notae sequenti modo delineari possunt. Hodie genus humanum in nova historiae suae aetate versatur in qua profundae et celeres mutationes ad universum orbem gradatim extenduntur. Ab hominis intelligentia et creativa industria excitatae in ipsum hominem recidunt, in eius iudicia et desideria individualia et collectiva, in eius modum cogitandi et agendi tum circa res tum circa homines. Ita iam de vera sociali et culturali transformatione loqui possumus, quae etiam in vitam religiosam redundat. Ut in quavis accretionis crisi contingit, haec transformatio non leves secumfert difficultates. Ita dum homo potentiam suam tam late extendit, eam tamen non semper ad suum servitium redigere valet. Proprii animi intimiora altius penetrare satagens, saepe de seipso magis incertus apparet. Leges vitae socialis pedetentim clarius detegens, de directione ei imprimenda anceps haeret. Numquam genus humanum tantis divitiis, facultatibus et potentia oeconomica abundavit, et tamen adhuc ingens pars incolarum orbis fame et egestate torquetur atque innumeri litterarum ignorantia plane laborant. Numquam homines tam acutum ut hodie sensum libertatis habuerunt, dum nova interea genera socialis et psychicae servitutis exsurgunt. Dum mundus suam unitatem necnon singulorum ab invicem dependentiam in necessaria solidarietate tam vivide persentit, viribus tamen inter se pugnantibus gravissime in opposita distrahitur; etenim acres dissensiones politicae, sociales, oeconomicae, raciales et ideologicae adhuc perserverant, nec periculum deest belli omnia usque ad ima destructuri. Dum idearum communicatio augetur, verba ipsa quibus magni momenti conceptus exprimuntur sensus sat diversos in distinctis ideologiis induunt. Tandem sedulo perfectior quaeritur temporalis ordinatio, quin spirituale incrementum pariter progrediatur. Tot implexis condicionibus affecti, plurimi coaevi nostri impediuntur quominus valore perennes vere dignoscant et simul cum noviter inventis rite componant; exinde, inter spem et angorem agitati, de praesenti rerum cursu sese interrogantes, inquietudine premuntur. Qui rerum cursus homines ad respondendum provocat, immo et constringit.
5 The present commotion of minds and the change of life conditions are connected with a wider transformation of things, by which it is brought about that in forming minds, mathematical and natural sciences, and those treating of man himself, acquire growing weight, while in ordering action, technical arts flowing from those sciences do the same. This scientific reason shapes cultural reason and ways of thinking in a different way than before. Technical arts progress to such an extent that they transform the face of the earth and even strive to subjugate extraterrestrial space. The human intellect also extends its dominion in a certain way over times: over the past by means of historical knowledge, over the future by the art of forecasting and planning. Progressing biological, psychological, and social sciences not only help man to better know himself, but also help him, by using technical methods, to directly exert influence on the life of societies. At the same time, the human race already more thinks about foreseeing and ordering its own demographic increase. History itself accelerates at so rapid a pace that individuals can scarcely follow it. The fate of human society becomes one and no longer as if dispersed among various histories. Thus the human race passes from a more static notion of the order of things to a more dynamic and evolutionary notion, whence arises a very great new complexity of problems, which provokes to new analyses and syntheses.
¶395
De profunde mutatis conditionibus
On the deeply changed conditions
Hodierna animorum commotio et in vitae condicionibus immutatio cum ampliori rerum transmutatione connectuntur, qua efficitur ut in mentibus efformandis scientiae mathematicae et naturales vel de ipso homine tractantes, in ordine vero agendi technicae artes ex illis scientiis profluentes, crescens pondus acquirant. Haec mens scientifica rationem culturalem modosque cogitandi aliter quam antea fingit. Technicae artes eo progrediuntur ut faciem terrae transforment etiam spatium ultraterrestre subigere conentur. Super tempora quoque humanus intellectus dominium suum quodammodo dilatat: in praeteritum ope cognitionis historicae, in futurum arte prospectiva et planificatione. Progredientes scientiae biologicae, psychologicae et sociales non solum homini ad meliorem sui cognitionem opem ferunt, sed ipsum etiam adiuvant ut, technicis methodis adhibitis, in vitam societatum directe influxum exerceat. Insimul genus humanum de proprio demographico incremento iam praevidendo et ordinando magisque cogitat. Ipsa historia tam rapido cursu acceleratur ut singuli eam vix prosequi valeant. Consortionis humanae sors una efficitur et non amplius inter varias velut historias dispergitur. Ita genus humanum a notione magis statica ordinis rerum ad notionem magis dynamicam atque evolutivam transit, unde quam maxima nascitur problematum nova complexio, quae ad novas analyses et synthesas provocat.
6 By this very fact, traditional local communities, such as patriarchal families, clans, tribes, villages, various groups, and relations of social association, experience more and more full changes. The industrial type of society is gradually diffused, leading some nations to economic opulence, and profoundly transforming concepts and conditions of social life established for centuries. Likewise, the cultivation of urban life and study increase, both through the growth of cities and their inhabitants, and through the movement by which urban life spreads to country folk. New and more suitable instruments of social communication contribute to knowing events and to very quickly and widely diffusing ways of thinking and feeling, exciting several connected repercussions. Nor is it to be made little of how many men, induced to migrate by various causes, change their manner of life. Thus the relations of man with his fellows incessantly multiply, and at the same time socialization itself introduces new relations, without always promoting congruent maturation of the person and personal relations (personalization). Such development indeed appears more clearly in nations which already enjoy the advantages of economic and technical progress, but also moves peoples still striving for development, who, for their regions, desire to obtain the benefits of industrialization and urbanization. Those peoples, especially devoted to older traditions, at the same time experience a movement toward a more mature and more personal exercise of liberty.
¶396
Changes in the social order
Mutationes in ordine sociali
Eo ipso communitates locales traditionales, uti sunt familiae patriarchales, clans, tribus, pagi, varii coetus et consortionis socialis necessitudines, pleniores in dies immutationes experiuntur. Typus industrialis societatis paulatim diffunditur, quasdam nationes ad oeconomicam opulentiam adducens, et notiones et condiciones vitae socialis a saeculis constitutas penitus transformans. Similiter vitae urbanae cultus ac studium augentur sive per urbium earumque incolarum augmentum, sive per motum quo vita urbana ad ruricolas dilatatur. Nova et aptiora communicationis socialis instrumenta ad eventus cognoscendos et ad modos cogitandi et sentiendi quam citissime latissimeque diffundendos conferunt, plures connexas repercussiones excitando. Nec parvipendendum est quot homines, ex variis causis, ad migrandum inducti, vitae suae rationem immutent. Sic necessitudines hominis cum similibus suis indesinenter multiplicantur ac simul ipsa socializatio novas necessitudines inducit, quin tamen congruentem personae maturationem et relationes vere personales (personalizationem) semper promoveat. Huiusmodi quidem evolutio clarius apparet in nationibus quae commodis progressus oeconomici et technici iam gaudent, sed populos quoque movet adhuc ad progressionem nitentes qui, pro suis regionibus beneficia industrializationis et urbanizationis obtinere cupiunt. Qui populi praesertim antiquioribus traditionibus addicti, simul motum experiuntur ad maturius magisque personale libertatis exercitium.
7 The change of mind and structures frequently calls into question good things received, especially among the young, who are not seldom impatient, even anguished rebels, and, conscious of their own importance in social life, wish to have their part in it more quickly. Hence it is not seldom that parents and educators experience ever greater difficulties in fulfilling their duties. Moreover, institutions, laws, and ways of thinking and feeling handed down by the fathers do not always seem to be well adapted to the present state of things; whence a grave disturbance arises in the manner and in the very norms of acting. Finally, new conditions also affect religious life itself. On the one hand, a sharper faculty of judging purifies it from magical concept of the world and from still wandering superstitions, and more and more demands a more personal and active adherence of faith; whence it comes about that not a few come to a more vivid sense of God. On the other hand, more frequent crowds practically withdraw from religion. Unlike in past times, to deny God or religion, or to withdraw from them, is no longer something unusual and individual: for today it is often presented as if an exigency of scientific progress or of a certain new humanism. All these things in many regions are expressed not only in philosophical opinions, but most widely affect literature, arts, human sciences and the interpretation of history, and the civil laws themselves, so that many are disturbed thereby.
¶397
Mutationes psychologicae, morales et religiosae
Psychological, moral, and religious changes
Mutatio mentis et structurarum bona recepta frequenter in controversiam vocat, maxime apud iuvenes qui non semel impatientes, immo angore rebelles fiunt et conscii de proprio momento in vita sociali, citius in eadem partes habere cupiunt. Exinde non raro parentes et educatores in muneribus suis adimplendis in dies maiores difficultates experiuntur. Instituta vero, leges atque modi cogitandi et sentiendi a maioribus tradita non semper statui rerum hodierno bene aptari videntur inde gravis perturbatio in modo et in ipsis agendi normis. Ipsam denique vitam religiosam novae condiciones afficiunt. Ex una parte acrior diiudicandi facultas eam a magico mundi conceptu et a superstitionibus adhuc vagantibus purificat atque magis personalem et actuosam adhaesionem fidei in dies exigit; quo fit ut non pauci ad vividiorem Dei sensum accedant. Ex altera vero parte crebriores turbae a religione practice discedunt. Secus ac transactis temporibus, Deum religionemve negare, aut ab iisdem abstrahere, non amplius quid insolitum et individuale sunt: hodie enim non raro quasi exigentia progressus scientifici vel cuiusdam novi humanismi exhibentur. Haec omnia in pluribus regionibus non tantum in philosophorum placitis exprimuntur, sed latissime litteras, artes, scientiarum humanarum et historiae interpretationem, ipsaque leges civiles afficiunt ita ut exinde multi perturbentur.
8 So rapid a change, often proceeding inordinately, and even the sharper consciousness of the discrepancies existing in the world, beget or increase contradictions and imbalances. In the person itself, an imbalance frequently arises between modern practical intellect and the theoretical manner of thinking, which cannot subdue the sum of its knowledge nor order it appropriately into syntheses. Likewise arises an imbalance between solicitude for practical efficiency and the exigencies of moral conscience, and also often between the conditions of collective life and the requirements of personal thought, indeed of contemplation. Finally, an imbalance arises between the specialization of human activity and the universal vision of things. In the family, however, discrepancies arise, whether from pressing demographic, economic, and social conditions, or from difficulties arising between succeeding generations, or from new social relations between men and women. Great discrepancies also arise between races, indeed between different orders of society; between opulent nations and those less powerful and needy; finally, between international institutions, arising from the desire for peace of peoples, and the ambition of spreading their own ideology and collective cupidities existing in nations or other groups. Hence mutual diffidences and enmities, conflicts and miseries, of which man himself is at once the cause and the victim.

De inaequilibriis in mundo hodierno
On the imbalances in the modern world
Tam rapida rerum mutatio inordinate saepe progrediens, immo et ipsa discrepantiarum in mundo vigentium acrior conscientia, contradictiones et inaequilibria gignunt vel augent. In ipsa persona frequentius oritur inaequilibrium inter modernum intellectum practicum et theoreticam cogitandi rationem, quae summam cognitionum suarum neque sibi subigere neque in syntheses apte ordinare valet. Oritur pariter inaequilibrium inter sollicitudinem efficientiae practicae et exigentias conscientiae moralis, necnon multoties inter condiciones vitae collectivas et requisita cogitationis personalis, immo et contemplationis. Oritur tandem inaequilibrium inter activitatis humanae specializationem et universalem rerum visionem. In familia autem discrepantiae oriuntur, sive ex prementibus condicionibus demographicis, oeconomicis et socialibus, sive ex difficultatibus inter generationes quae sibi subsequuntur exsurgentibus, sive ex novis necessitudinibus socialibus inter viros ac mulieres. Magnae oriuntur etiam discrepantiae inter stirpes, immo inter varii generis societatis ordines; inter nationes opulentas et minus valentes egentesque; denique, inter instituta internationalia, ex pacis desiderio populorum exorta, et ambitionem propriae ideologiae disseminandae nec non cupiditates collectivas in nationibus aliisve coetibus exsistentes. Inde mutuae diffidentiae et inimicitiae, conflictationes et aerumnae, quarum ipse homo simul causa et victima.
9 In the meantime, the persuasion grows that the human race can and ought not only more and more to strengthen its dominion over created things; but also that it is its part to establish a political, social, and economic order which may better and better serve man and help individuals and groups to affirm and cultivate their own dignity. Hence very many most sharply demand those goods by which, with vivid consciousness they judge themselves deprived by injustice or unequal distribution. Nations on the way to progress, like those recently made independent, desire to participate in the goods of modern civilization not only in the political but also in the economic field, and freely to exercise their parts in the world, while nevertheless the distance of the same increases and at the same time often even economic dependence on other richer nations progressing more quickly. Peoples pressed by hunger call upon opulent peoples. Women claim for themselves, where they have not yet attained it, equality de jure and de facto with men. Workers and farmers desire not only to procure the necessities of life by labor, but also to cultivate the gifts of their own person, indeed to exercise their own parts in ordering economic, social, political, and cultural life. For the first time in human history, all peoples already persuade themselves that the benefits of culture can and ought to be truly extended to all. Beneath all these exigencies, however, lies a deeper and more universal aspiration: the person and the group thirst for a full and free life, worthy of man, subjecting to their own service all things which the modern world can so abundantly provide for them. Moreover, nations strive more and more strongly to attain some universal community. Which things being so, the modern world shows itself at once powerful and weak, capable of achieving the best or the worst, while the way to freedom or servitude, to progress or regression, to fraternity or hatred lies open before it. Moreover, man becomes conscious that it is his part rightly to direct the forces which he himself has aroused and which can oppress him or serve him. Hence he asks himself.

De appetitionibus universalioribus generis humani
On the more universal aspirations of the human race
Interea crescit persuasio genus humanum non tantum imperium suum super res creatas in dies magis roborare posse ac debere; sed insuper eius esse ordinem politicum, socialem et oeconomicum statuere qui in dies melius homini inserviat et singulos ac coetus adiuvet ad dignitatem sibi propriam affirmandam et excolendam. Hinc plurimi acerrime exigunt illa bona quibus, per iniustitiam vel non aequam distributionem, orbatos se esse vivida conscientia iudicant. Nationes in via progressus sicut illae recenter sui iuris factae, bona civilizationis hodiernae non tantum in campo politico sed etiam oeconomico participare et libere partibus suis in mundo fungi cupiunt, dum tamen in dies augetur earumdem distantia simul ac persaepe dependentia etiam oeconomica ab aliis ditioribus nationibus citius progredientibus. Populi fame pressi populos opulentiores interpellant. Mulieres sibi vindicant, ubi eam nondum sunt consecutae, paritatem de iure et de facto cum viris. Opifices et ruricolae non solum victui necessaria comparare, sed laborando dotes suae personae excolere, immo in ordinanda vita oeconomica, sociali, politica et culturali suas partes agere volunt. Nunc primum in historia humana universi populi iam persuasum sibi habent culturae beneficia reapse ad cunctos extendi posse ac debere. Sub omnibus autem istis exigentiis latet profundior et universalior appetito: personae scilicet atque coetus plenam atque liberam vitam, homine dignam, sitiunt, omnia quae hodiernus mundus eis tam abundanter praebere potest proprio servitio subicientes. Nationes praeterea in dies fortius enituntur ut universalem quamdam communitatem assequantur. Quae cum ita sint, mundus hodiernus simul potentem ac debilem se exhibet, capacem optima vel pessima patrandi, dum ipsi ad libertatem aut servitutem, ad progressum aut regressum, ad fraternitatem aut odium prostat via. Praeterea, homo conscius fit ipsius esse recte dirigere vires, quas ipse suscitavit et quae eum opprimere aut ei servire possunt. Unde seipsum interrogat.
10 Indeed, the imbalances under which the modern world labors are connected with that fundamental imbalance which is rooted in the heart of man. For in man himself many elements contend with one another. For while on the one hand, as a creature, he experiences himself limited in many ways, on the other hand he feels himself unlimited in his desires and called to a higher life. Attracted by many solicitations, he is forced always to choose among them and to renounce some. Indeed, weak and sinful, he not seldom does that which he does not wish and does not do that which he would wish. Hence he suffers division in himself, from which also so many and so great discords in society arise. Many indeed, whose life is infected with practical materialism, are turned away from a clear perception of this dramatic state, or, oppressed by misery, are prevented from considering it. Many think they find rest in the interpretation of things proposed in many ways. Some, however, expect the true and full liberation of the human race from human effort alone, and persuade themselves that the future kingdom of man over the earth will fulfill all the desires of his heart. Nor are there lacking those who, despairing of the meaning of life, praise the audacity of those who, judging human existence to be deprived of all proper meaning, strive to give it all meaning from their own genius alone. Nevertheless, facing the present development of the world, more and more numerous become those who either pose the most fundamental questions or perceive them with new acuteness: what is man? What is the meaning of pain, of evil, of death, which, although so much progress has been made, continue to exist? To what purpose are those victories acquired at so great a price? What can man bring to society, what expect from it? What will follow after this earthly life? The Church believes that Christ, dead and raised for all, gives light and strength to man through His Spirit, that he may be able to respond to his highest calling; nor is any other name given under heaven to men, in which they ought to be saved. Likewise it believes that the key, center, and end of all human history is found in its Lord and Master. Moreover, the Church affirms that beneath all changes there are many things which do not change, which have their ultimate foundation in Christ, who is the same yesterday, today, and forever. Under the light of Christ, therefore, the image of the invisible God, the Firstborn of every creature, the Council intends to speak to all to illustrate the mystery of man and to cooperate in finding the solution of the principal questions of our time.

De profundioribus interrogationibus generis humani
On the deeper questions of the human race
Revera inaequilibria quibus laborat mundus hodiernus cum inaequilibrio illo fumdamentaliori connectuntur, quod in hominis corde radicatur. In ipso enim homine plura elementa sibi invicem oppugnant. Dum enim una ex parte, utpote creatura, multipliciter sese limitatum experitur, ex altera vero in desideriis suis illimitatum et ad superiorem vitam vocatum se sentit. Multis sollicitationibus attractus, iugiter inter eas seligere et quibusdam renuntiare cogitur. Immo, infirmus ac peccator, non raro illud quod non vult facit et illud quod facere vellet non facit. Unde in seipso divisionem patitur, ex qua etiam tot ac tantae discordiae in societate oriuntur. Plurimi sane, quorum vita materialismo practico inficitur, a clara huiusmodi dramatici status perceptione avertuntur, vel autem, miseria oppressi, impediuntur quominus illum considerent. Multi in interpretatione rerum multifarie proposita quietem se invenire existimant. Quidam vero a solo conatu humano veram plenamque generis humani liberationem expectant, sibique persuasum habent futurum regnum hominis super terram omnia vota cordis eius expleturum esse. Nec desunt qui, de sensu vitae desperantes, audaciam laudant eorum qui, exsistentiam humanam omnis significationis propriae expertem existimantes, ei totam significationem ex solo proprio ingenio conferre nituntur. Attamen, coram hodierna mundi evolutione, in dies numerosiores fiunt qui quaestiones maxime fundamentales vel ponunt vel nova acuitate persentiunt: quid est homo? Quinam est sensus doloris, mali, mortis, quae, quamquam tantus progressus factus est, subsistere pergunt? Ad quid victoriae illae tanto pretio acquisitae? Quid societati homo afferre, quid ab ea exspectare potest? Quid post vitam hanc terrestrem subsequetur? Credit autem Ecclesia Christum, pro omnibus mortuum et resuscitatum, homini lucem et vires per Spiritum suum praebere ut ille summae suae vocationi respondere possit; nec aliud nomen sub caelo datum esse hominibus, in quo oporteat eos salvos fieri. Similiter credit clavem, centrum et finem totius humanae historiae in Domino ac Magistro suo inveniri. Affirmat insuper Ecclesia omnibus mutationibus multa subesse quae non mutantur, quaeque fundamentum suum ultimum in Christo habent, qui est heri, hodie, Ipse et in saecula. Sub lumine ergo Christi, Imaginis Dei invisibilis, Primogeniti omnis creaturae, Concilium, ad mysterium hominis illustrandum atque ad cooperandum in solutionem praecipuarum quaestionum nostri temporis inveniendam, omnes alloqui intendit.
11 The people of God, moved by faith, by which it believes that it is led by the Spirit of the Lord who fills the earth, in events, exigencies, and desires, in which it shares together with the other men of our age, strives to discern what in them are the true signs of the presence or counsel of God. For faith illuminates all things with new light and manifests the divine purpose concerning the whole vocation of man, and therefore directs the mind to solutions fully human. The Council intends first of all to judge under this light those values which are most esteemed today, and to refer them to their divine source. For these values, as they proceed from the human genius divinely given to it, are very good; but often from the corruption of the human heart they are twisted from their due order, so that they need purification.

DE ECCLESIA ET VOCATIONE HOMINIS
Part One
ON THE CHURCH AND THE VOCATION OF MAN
Responding to the impulses of the Spirit
Impulsionibus Spiritus respondendum
Populus Dei, fide motus, qua credit se a Spiritu Domini duci qui replet orbem terrarum, in eventibus, exigentiis atque optatis, quorum una cum ceteris nostrae aetatis hominibus partem habet, quaenam in illis sint vera signa praesentiae vel consilii Dei, discernere satagit. Fides enim omnia novo lumine illustrat et divinum propositum de integra hominis vocatione manifestat, ideoque ad solutiones plene humanas mentem dirigit. Concilium imprimis illos valores, qui hodie maxime aestimantur, sub hoc lumine diiudicare et ad fontem suum divinum referre intendit. Hi enim valores, prout ex hominis ingenio eidem divinitus collato procedunt, valde boni sunt; sed ex corruptione humani cordis a sua debita ordinatione non raro detorquentur, ita ut purificatione indigeant.
What does the Church think of man? What seems to be commended for building modern society? What is the ultimate meaning of human activity in the whole world? An answer is expected to these questions. From it will appear more clearly that the people of God and the human race, in which it is inserted, render service to one another, so that the mission of the Church shows itself to be religious and from this very fact supremely human.
¶402
Quid Ecclesia de homine sentit? Quaenam ad societatem hodiernam aedificandam commendanda videntur? Quaenam est significatio ultima humanae navitatis in universo mundo? Ad has quaestiones responsio exspectatur. Exinde luculentius apparebit populum Dei et genus humanum, cui ille inseritur, servitium sibi mutuo praestare, ita ut Ecclesiae missio religiosam et ex hoc ipso summe humanam se exhibeat.
12 According to the almost unanimous opinion of believers and unbelievers, all things which are on earth are to be ordered to man, as to their center and summit. But what is man? He has put forth and puts forth many opinions about himself, various and even contrary, by which he often either exalts himself as an absolute rule or sinks to desperation, hence hesitating and anxious. The Church, feeling these difficulties, instructed by God revealing, can bring an answer to them, by which the true condition of man is delineated, his infirmities explained, and at the same time his dignity and vocation rightly acknowledged. For the sacred Scriptures teach that man was created to the image of God, capable of knowing and loving his Creator, constituted by Him as lord over all earthly creatures, that he might rule them and use them, glorifying God. "What is man that thou art mindful of him? or the son of man that thou visitest him? Thou hast made him a little lower than the angels, thou hast crowned him with glory and honor, and hast set him over the works of thy hands. Thou hast put all things under his feet" (Ps 8:5-7). But God did not create man alone: for from the beginning "He created them male and female" (Gen 1:27), the association of which constitutes the first form of communion of persons. For man by his intimate nature is a social being, and can neither live nor expand his gifts without relations with others. God, therefore, as we read again in sacred page, "saw all the things that He had made, and they were very good" (Gen 1:31).

CAPUT I. DE HUMANAE PERSONAE DIGNITATE
CHAPTER I. ON THE DIGNITY OF THE HUMAN PERSON
De homine ad imaginem Dei
On man made to the image of God
Secundum credentium et non credentium fere concordem sententiam, omnia quae in terra sunt ad hominem, tamquam ad centrum suum et culmen, ordinanda sunt. Quid est autem homo? Multas opiniones de seipso protulit et profert, varias et etiam contrarias, quibus saepe vel se tamquam absolutam regulam exaltat vel usque ad desperationem deprimit, exinde anceps et anxius. Quas quidem difficultates Ecclesia persentiens, a Deo revelante instructa eisdem responsum afferre potest, quo vera hominis condicio delineetur, explanentur eius infirmitates, simulque eius dignitas et vocatio recte agnosci possint. Sacrae enim Litterae docent hominem ad imaginem Dei creatum esse, capacem suum Creatorem cognoscendi et amandi, ab eo tamquam dominum super omnes creaturas terrenas constitutum, ut eas regeret, eisque uteretur, glorificans Deum. Quid est homo quod memor es eius? aut filius hominis, quoniam visitas eum? Minuisti eum paulo minus ab angelis, gloria et honore coronasti eum, et constituisti eum super opera manuum tuarum. Omnia subiecisti sub pedibus eius (Ps 8,5-7). At Deus non creavit hominem solum: nam inde a primordiis masculum et feminam creavit eos (Gn 1,27), quorum consociatio primam formam efficit communionis personarum. Homo etenim ex intima sua natura ens sociale est, atque sine relationibus cum aliis nec vivere nec suas dotes expandere potest. Deus igitur, sicut iterum in sacra Pagina legimus, vidit cuncta quae fecerat, et erant valde bona (Gn 1,31).
13 Constituted in justice by God, man, however, the Evil One persuading, from the beginning of history abused his freedom, raising himself against God and desiring to attain his end outside God. "When they knew God, they have not glorified him as God, but their foolish heart was darkened, and they served the creature rather than the Creator" (Rom 1:21-25). What divine revelation makes known to us, concords with experience itself. For man, looking into his heart, finds himself inclined also to evil and sunk in manifold evils, which cannot proceed from his good Creator. Often refusing to acknowledge God as his principle, he also disrupts the due order toward his ultimate end, as well as his whole ordering toward himself, toward other men, and toward all created things. Therefore man is divided in himself. Therefore the whole life of men, both individual and collective, shows itself as a struggle, and indeed a dramatic one, between good and evil, between light and darkness. Indeed, man finds himself incapable of effectively conquering the assaults of evil by himself, so that each feels himself as if bound with chains. But the Lord Himself came to free and strengthen man, renewing him from within and casting out the prince of this world (cf. Jn 12:31), who held him in the slavery of sin. Sin, however, diminishes man himself, repelling him from the fullness to be attained. In the light of this Revelation, both the sublime vocation and the profound misery which men experience find their ultimate reason.

De peccato
On sin
In iustitia a Deo constitutus, homo tamen, suadente Maligno, inde ab exordio historiae, libertate sua abusus est, seipsum contra Deum erigens et finem suum extra Deum attingere cupiens. Cum cognovissent Deum, non sicut Deum glorificaverunt, sed obscuratum est insipiens cor eorum et servierunt creaturae potius quam Creatori. Quod Revelatione divina nobis innotescit, cum ipsa experientia concordat. Nam homo, cor suum inspiciens, etiam ad malum inclinatum se comperit et in multiplicibus malis demersum, quae a bono suo Creatore provenire non possunt. Deum tamquam principium suum saepe agnoscere renuens, etiam debitum ordinem ad finem suum ultimum, simul ac totam suam sive erga seipsum sive erga alios homines et omnes res creatas ordinationem disrupit. Ideo in seipso divisus est homo. Quapropter tota vita hominum, sive singularis sive collectiva, ut luctationem et quidem dramaticam se exhibet inter bonum et malum, inter lucem et tenebras. Immo incapacem se invenit homo per seipsum mali impugnationes efficaciter debellandi, ita ut unusquisque se quasi catenis vinctum sentiat. At ipse Dominus venit ut hominem liberaret et confortaret, eum interius renovans ac principem huius mundi (cf. Jn 12,31) foras eiiciens qui eum in servitute peccati retinebat. Peccatum autem minuit ipsum hominem, a plenitudine consequenda eum repellens. In lumine huius Revelationis simul sublimis vocatio et profunda miseria, quas homines experiuntur, rationem suam ultimam inveniunt.
14 One in body and soul, man by his very bodily condition gathers into himself the elements of the material world, so that through him they attain their summit and lift up their voice to the free praise of the Creator. Therefore man is not allowed to despise bodily life, but on the contrary he is bound to hold his body, as created by God and to be raised up on the last day, good and worthy of honor. Wounded by sin, however, he experiences rebellions of the body. The very dignity of man therefore demands that he glorify God in his body, and not allow it to serve the evil inclinations of his heart. Man, however, is not deceived when he acknowledges himself to be superior to bodily things, and considers himself not only as a particle of nature or an anonymous element of human society. For by interiority he exceeds the totality of things: he returns to this deep interiority when he turns to his heart, where God awaits him, who searches hearts, and where he himself under the eyes of God decides concerning his own lot. Therefore, acknowledging a spiritual and immortal soul in himself, he is not deluded by a fallacious fiction, flowing from physical and social conditions alone, but on the contrary attains the deep truth of things.

De hominis constitutione
On the constitution of man
Corpore et anima unus, homo per ipsam suam corporalem condicionem elementa mundi materialis in se colligit, ita ut, per ipsum, fastigium suum attingant et ad liberam Creatoris laudem vocem attollant. Vitam ergo corporalem homini despicere non licet, sed e contra ipse corpus suum, utpote a Deo creatum et ultima die resuscitandum, bonum et honore dignum habere tenetur. Peccato tamen vulneratus, corporis rebelliones experitur. Ipsa igitur dignitas hominis postulat ut Deum glorificet in corpore suo, neve illud pravis cordis sui inclinationibus inservire sinat. Homo vero non fallitur, cum se rebus corporalibus superiorem agnoscit, et non tantum ut particulam naturae aut anonymum elementum civitatis humanae seipsum considerat. Interioritate enim sua universitatem rerum excedit: ad hanc profundam interioritatem redit, quando convertitur ad cor, ubi Deus eum exspectat, qui corda scrutatur, et ubi ipse sub oculis Dei de propria sorte decernit. Itaque, animam spiritualem et immortalem in seipso agnoscens, non fallaci figmento illuditur, a physicis tantum et socialibus condicionibus fluente, sed e contra ipsam profundam rei veritatem attingit.
15 Man judges rightly, partaking of the light of the divine mind, that by his intellect he surpasses the totality of things. By tirelessly exercising his genius through the ages, he has truly advanced in empirical sciences, technical and liberal arts. In our times, however, especially in investigating and subjecting the material world to himself, he has obtained distinguished successes. Always, however, he has sought and found deeper truth. For the intellect is not confined to mere phenomena, but can attain intelligible reality with true certainty, even if from the sequel of sin it is partly obscured and weakened. Finally, the intellectual nature of the human person is perfected and is to be perfected by wisdom, which gently attracts the mind of man to seek and love true goods, and by which man, imbued, is led through visible things to invisible. Our age, however, more than past ages, needs such wisdom that all new things discovered by man may become more humane. For the future fate of the world is endangered unless wiser men are raised up. Moreover, it is to be noted that many nations, poorer indeed in economic goods, but richer in wisdom, can offer outstanding benefit to others. By the gift of the Holy Spirit, man approaches to contemplate and savor the mystery of the divine plan by faith.

De dignitate intellectus, de veritate et de sapientia
On the dignity of the intellect, on truth, and on wisdom
Recte iudicat homo, divinae mentis lumen participans, se intellectu suo universitatem rerum superare. Ingenium suum per saecula impigre exercendo ipse in scientiis empiricis, artibus technicis et liberalibus sane profecit. Nostris autem temporibus in mundo materiali praesertim investigando et sibi subiiciendo egregios obtinuit successus. Semper tamen profundiorem veritatem quaesivit et invenit. Intelligentia enim non ad sola phaenomena coarctatur, sed realitatem intelligibilem cum vera certitudine adipisci valet, etiamsi, ex sequela peccati, ex parte obscuratur et debilitatur. Humanae tandem personae intellectualis natura per sapientiam perficitur et perficienda est, quae mentem hominis ad vera bonaque inquirenda ac diligenda suaviter attrahit, et qua imbutus homo per visibilia ad invisibilia adducitur. Aetas autem nostra, magis quam saecula anteacta, tali sapientia indiget ut humaniora fiant quaecumque nova ab homine deteguntur. Periclitatur enim sors futura mundi nisi sapientiores suscitentur homines. Insuper notandum est plures nationes, bonis quidem oeconomicis pauperiores, sapientia vero ditiores, ceteris eximium emolumentum praestare posse. Spiritus Sancti dono, homo ad mysterium consilii divini contemplandum et sapiendum fide accedit.
16 In the depths of his conscience, man discovers a law which he does not impose on himself, but to which he ought to obey, and whose voice, always calling him to love and do good and avoid evil, when necessary sounds in the ears of his heart: do this, shun that. For man has a law inscribed in his heart by God, to obey which is his very dignity and according to which he himself will be judged. Conscience is the most secret nucleus and sanctuary of man, in which he is alone with God, whose voice resounds in his intimate being. Conscience makes known in a wonderful way that law which is fulfilled in the love of God and neighbor. By fidelity to conscience, Christians are joined with other men to seek truth and to solve in truth the many moral problems which arise both in the life of individuals and in social association. Therefore, the more right conscience prevails, the more persons and groups withdraw from blind arbitrariness and strive to conform to objective norms of morality. Yet not seldom it happens that conscience errs from invincible ignorance, without losing its dignity. But this cannot be said when man cares little about seeking truth and good, and conscience gradually becomes almost blinded from the habit of sin.
¶407
De dignitate conscientiae moralis
On the dignity of the moral conscience
In imo conscientiae legem homo detegit, quam ipse sibi non dat, sed cui obedire debet, et cuius vox, semper ad bonum amandum et faciendum ac malum vitandum eum advocans, ubi oportet auribus cordis sonat: fac hoc, illud devita. Nam homo legem in corde suo a Deo inscriptam habet, cui parere ipsa dignitas eius est et secundum quam ipse iudicabitur. Conscientia est nucleus secretissimus atque sacrarium hominis, in quo solus est cum Deo, cuius vox resonat in intimo eius. Conscientia modo mirabili illa lex innotescit, quae in Dei et proximi dilectione adimpletur. Fidelitate erga conscientiam christiani cum ceteris hominibus coniunguntur ad veritatem inquirendam et tot problemata moralia, quae tam in vita singulorum quam in sociali consortione exsurgunt, in veritate solvenda. Quo magis ergo conscientia recta praevalet, eo magis personae et coetus a caeco arbitrio recedunt et normis obiectivis moralitatis conformari satagunt. Non raro tamen evenit ex ignorantia invincibili conscientiam errare, quin inde suam dignitatem amittat. Quod autem dici nequit cum homo de vero ac bono inquirendo parum curat, et conscientia ex peccati consuetudine paulatim fere obcaecatur.
17 But only freely can man turn to good, which freedom our contemporaries greatly esteem and ardently pursue: and rightly indeed. Yet they often foster it in a depraved way, as license to do whatever pleases, even evil. True freedom, however, is an excellent sign of the divine image in man. For God willed to leave man in the hand of his own counsel, so that he may seek his Creator spontaneously and freely attain full and blessed perfection by adhering to Him. The dignity of man therefore requires that he act according to conscious and free choice, personally moved and induced from within, and not under blind internal impulse or mere external coercion. Man, however, obtains such dignity when, freeing himself from all captivity of passions, he pursues his end in the free choice of good and effectively and skillfully procures suitable means for himself. This ordering toward God, man's freedom wounded by sin, can only make fully active by the help of God's grace. But each will have to render an account of his own life before the tribunal of God, according as he has done either good or evil.
¶408
De praestantia libertatis
On the excellence of freedom
At nonnisi libere homo ad bonum se convertere potest, quam libertatem coaevi nostri magni faciunt ardenterque prosequuntur: et recte sane. Saepe tamen eam pravo modo fovent, tamquam licentiam quidquid faciendi dummodo delectet, etiam malum. Vera autem libertas eximium est divinae imaginis in homine signum. Voluit enim Deus hominem relinquere in manu consilii sui, ita ut Creatorem suum sponte quaerat et libere ad plenam et beatam perfectionem ei inhaerendo perveniat. Dignitas igitur hominis requirit ut secundum consciam et liberam electionem agat, personaliter scilicet ab intra motus et inductus, et non sub caeco impulsu interno vel sub mera externa coactione. Talem vero dignitatem obtinet homo cum, sese ab omni passionum captivitate liberans, finem suum in boni libera electione persequitur et apta subsidia efficaciter ac sollerti industria sibi procurat. Quam ordinationem ad Deum libertas hominis, a peccato vulnerata, nonnisi gratia Dei adiuvante, plene actuosam efficere potest. Unicuique autem ante tribunal Dei propriae vitae ratio reddenda erit, prout ipse sive bonum sive malum gesserit.
18 Before death, the enigma of the human condition becomes greatest. Not only is man tormented by pain and the progressive dissolution of the body, but also, indeed more, by the fear of perpetual extinction. Rightly, by the instinct of his heart, he judges and repels the total ruin and definitive end of his person. The seed of eternity which he bears in himself, irreducible to matter alone, rises up against death. All the efforts of technical art, although very useful, cannot calm the anxiety of man: for prolonged biological longevity cannot satisfy the desire for further life, which ineluctably lies in his heart. While before death all imagination fails, the Church, however, taught by divine Revelation, affirms that man was created by God for a blessed end, beyond the limits of earthly misery. Moreover, bodily death, from which man would have been exempted if he had not sinned, the Christian faith teaches will be overcome, when man, his salvation lost by his fault, is restored by the all-powerful and merciful Savior. For God called and calls man that he may adhere to Him in perpetual communion of incorruptible divine life with his whole nature. Christ, freeing man from death by His death, attained this victory by rising to life. Therefore, to any man reflecting, faith, offered with solid arguments, offers a response in his anxiety concerning his future lot; at the same time it gives him the faculty of communicating with beloved brothers already taken by death in Christ, giving hope that they have attained true life with God.
¶409
De mysterio mortis
On the mystery of death
Coram morte aenigma condicionis humanae maximum evadit. Non tantum cruciatur homo dolore et corporis dissolutione progrediente, sed etiam, immo magis, perpetuae extinctionis timore. Recte autem instinctu cordis sui iudicat, cum totalem ruinam et definitivum exitum suae personae abhorret et respuit. Semen aeternitatis quod in se gerit, ad solam materiam cum irreductibile sit, contra mortem insurgit. Omnia technicae artis molimina, licet perutilia, anxietatem hominis sedare non valent: prorogata enim biologica longaevitas illi ulterioris vitae desiderio satisfacere nequit, quod cordi eius ineluctabiliter inest. Dum coram morte omnis imaginatio deficit, Ecclesia tamen, Revelatione divina edocta, hominem ad beatum finem, ultra terrestris miseriae limites, a Deo creatum esse affirmat. Mors insuper corporalis, a qua homo si non peccasset subtractus fuisset, fides christiana docet fore ut vincatur, cum homo in salutem, culpa sua perditam, ab omnipotente et miserante Salvatore restituetur. Deus enim hominem vocavit et vocat ut Ei in perpetua incorruptibilis vitae divinae communione tota sua natura adhaereat. Quam victoriam Christus, hominem a morte per mortem suam liberando, ad vitam resurgens adeptus est. Cuicumque igitur recogitanti homini, fides, cum solidis argumentis oblata, in eius anxietate de sorte futura responsum offert; simulque facultatem praebet cum dilectis fratribus iam morte praereptis in Christo communicandi, spem conferens eos veram vitam apud Deum adeptos esse.
19 An excellent reason of human dignity consists in the vocation of man to communion with God. For man is invited to converse with God from his very origin: for he does not exist unless because, created by God from love, he is always conserved from love; nor does he live fully according to truth unless he freely acknowledges that love and commits himself to his Creator. Many, however, of our contemporaries do not at all perceive this intimate and vital connection with God, or explicitly reject it, so that atheism is to be counted among the most serious things of this time and to be subjected to more diligent examination. The word atheism designates phenomena very different among themselves. For while by some God is expressly denied, others think that man can assert nothing at all about Him; others submit the question of God to such a method of examination that it seems to lack sense. Many, unduly transgressing the limits of the positive sciences, either contend that all things are explained by this merely scientific reason, or on the contrary admit no absolute truth at all. Some so exalt man that faith in God almost becomes enervated, more inclined, as it seems, to the affirmation of man than to the denial of God. Others so conceive of God that the figment which they reject is by no means the God of the Gospel. Others do not even approach questions concerning God, since they seem to experience no religious anxiety nor perceive why religion should now be of concern to them. Atheism, moreover, not seldom arises either from a violent protest against evil in the world, or from the characteristic of the absolute unduly assigned to certain human goods, so that these are held in the place of God. Modern civilization itself, not from itself, but as too entangled in earthly things, can often make access to God more difficult. Indeed, those who voluntarily strive to banish God from their hearts and to avoid religious questions, not following the dictate of their conscience, are not without fault; yet even believers themselves often bear a certain responsibility concerning this. For atheism, considered integrally, is not something original, but arises rather from various causes, among which is also a critical reaction against religions and indeed, in some regions, especially against the Christian religion. Wherefore, in this genesis of atheism, believers can have no small part, insofar as, by neglecting the education of faith, or by a fallacious exposition of doctrine, or also by defects in their own religious, moral, and social life, they are to be said rather to veil than to reveal the genuine face of God and religion.
¶410
De formis et radicibus atheismi
On the forms and roots of atheism
Dignitatis humanae eximia ratio in vocatione hominis ad communionem cum Deo consistit. Ad colloquium cum Deo iam inde ab ortu suo invitatur homo: non enim exsistit, nisi quia, a Deo ex amore creatus, semper ex amore conservatur; nec plene secundum veritatem vivit, nisi amorem illum libere agnoscat et Creatori suo se committat. Multi tamen ex coaevis nostris hanc intimam ac vitalem cum Deo coniunctionem nequaquam perspiciunt aut explicite reiiciunt, ita ut atheismus inter gravissimas huius temporis res adnumerandus sit ac diligentiori examini subiiciendus. Voce atheismi phaenomena inter se valde diversa designantur. Dum enim a quibusdam Deus expresse negatur, alii censent hominem nihil omnino de Eo asserere posse; alii vero quaestionem de Deo tali methodo examini subiiciunt, ut illa sensu carere videatur. Multi, scientiarum positivarum limites indebite praetergressi, aut omnia hac sola scientifica ratione explicari contendunt aut e contra nullam omnino veritatem absolutam iam admittunt. Quidam hominem tantopere exaltant, ut fides in Deum quasi enervis fiat, magis proclives, ut videntur, ad affirmationem hominis quam ad Dei negationem. Alii Deum sibi ita effingunt, ut illud figmentum, quod repudiant, nullo modo sit Deus Evangelii. Alii quaestiones de Deo ne aggrediuntur quidem, quippe qui inquietudinem religiosam non experiri videantur nec percipiant quare de religione iam sibi curandum sit. Atheismus praeterea non raro oritur sive ex violenta contra malum in mundo protestatione, sive ex nota ipsius absoluti quibusdam humanis bonis indebite adiudicata, ita ut ista iam loco Dei habeantur. Ipsa civilizatio hodierna, non ex se, sed utpote nimis rebus terrestribus intricata accessum ad Deum saepe difficiliorem reddere potest. Sane qui voluntarie Deum a corde suo arcere et quaestiones religiosas devitare conantur, dictamen conscientiae suae non secuti, culpae expertes non sunt; attamen et ipsi credentes quamdam de hoc responsabilitatem saepe ferunt. Atheismus enim, integre consideratus, non est quid originarium, sed potius ex diversis causis oritur, inter quas adnumeratur etiam reactio critica contra religiones et quidem, in nonnullis regionibus, praesertim contra religionem christianam. Quapropter in hac atheismi genesi partem non parvam habere possunt credentes, quatenus, neglecta fidei educatione, vel fallaci doctrinae expositione, vel etiam vitae suae religiosae, moralis ac socialis defectibus, Dei et religionis genuinum vultum potius velare quam revelare dicendi sint.
20 Modern atheism often also presents a systematic form, which, besides other causes, carries the desire for the autonomy of man to such an extent that it raises difficulty against any dependence on God. Those who profess such atheism contend that freedom consists in this, that man is his own end, the sole artificer and demiurge of his own history: which they think cannot be reconciled with the acknowledgment of the Lord, the author and end of all things, or at least renders such affirmation altogether superfluous. This doctrine can be favored by the sense of power which modern technical progress gives to man. Among the forms of modern atheism, that is not to be passed over which expects the liberation of man especially from his economic and social liberation. To this liberation, religion of its nature opposes, they contend, insofar as, raising the hope of man in a false future life, it would deter him from building the earthly city. Hence the supporters of such doctrine, when they come to the governance of the republic, vehemently oppress religion, diffusing atheism even by using those means of pressure which public power has, especially in the education of the young.
¶411
De atheismo systematico
On systematic atheism
Atmosphaera moderna atheismi moderni saepe formam etiam systematicam praebet, quae, praeter alias causas, optatum autonomiae hominis eo usque perducit ut contra qualemcumque a Deo dependentiam difficultatem suscitet. Qui talem atheismum profitentur, libertatem in eo esse contendunt quod homo sibi ipse sit finis, propriae suae historiae solus artifex et demiurgus: quod componi non posse autumant cum agnitione Domini, omnium rerum auctoris et finis, vel saltem talem affirmationem plane superfluam reddere. Cui doctrinae favere potest sensus potentiae quem hodiernus progressus technicus homini confert. Inter formas hodierni atheismi illa non praetermittenda est, quae liberationem hominis praesertim ex eius liberatione oeconomica et sociali exspectat. Huic autem liberationi religionem natura sua obstare contendit, quatenus, in futuram fallacemque vitam spem hominis erigens, ipsum a civitatis terrestris aedificatione deterreret. Unde fautores talis doctrinae, ubi ad regimen reipublicae accedunt, religionem vehementer oppugnant, atheismum diffundentes etiam adhibitis, praesertim in iuvenum educatione, illis pressionis mediis, quibus potestas publica pollet.
21 The Church, faithfully devoted both to God and to men, cannot cease to reprove with all firmness, as she has reproved before, those pernicious doctrines and actions which contradict reason and common human experience and cast man down from his innate excellence. Yet she strives to discover the hidden causes of the denial of God in the mind of atheists, and, conscious of the gravity of the questions which atheism excites and led by charity toward all men, judges that they are to be subjected to a serious and deeper examination. The Church holds that the acknowledgment of God in no way opposes the dignity of man, since such dignity is founded and perfected in God Himself: for man is constituted intelligent and free in society by God the Creator; but especially he is called as a son to the communion of God and to participation in His happiness. She teaches, moreover, that through eschatological hope the importance of earthly duties is not diminished, but rather their fulfillment is supported by new motives. On the contrary, if the divine foundation and the hope of eternal life are lacking, the dignity of man is gravely injured, as often appears today, and the enigmas of life and death, of guilt and sorrow, remain without solution, so that men not seldom sink into despair. Every man, however, remains to himself an unsolved question, obscurely perceived. For no one at certain moments, especially in greater events of life, can altogether avoid the aforesaid question. To this question, God alone fully and with all certainty gives an answer, who calls man to deeper thought and humbler inquiry. The remedy for atheism, however, is to be expected both from an aptly set forth doctrine and from the whole life of the Church and its members. For it is the Church's part to render God the Father and His incarnate Son present and as it were visible, by ceaselessly renewing and purifying herself under the guidance of the Holy Spirit. This is obtained especially by the testimony of a living and mature faith, namely educated to be able clearly to perceive difficulties and to overcome them. A splendid testimony of this faith very many martyrs have given and give. This faith ought to manifest its fruitfulness, penetrating the whole life of believers, even the profane, and moving them to justice and love, especially toward the needy. Finally, the fraternal charity of the faithful, who unanimous in spirit collaborate for the faith of the Gospel and show themselves as a sign of unity, most greatly contributes to manifesting the presence of God. The Church, however, although she altogether rejects atheism, nevertheless sincerely professes that all men, believers and unbelievers, ought to contribute to building up this world, in which they commonly live, which certainly cannot be done without a sincere and prudent dialogue. She therefore complains of the discrimination between believers and unbelievers which certain rulers of cities, not acknowledging the fundamental rights of the human person, unjustly introduce. For believers she demands active freedom that they may be permitted to build the temple of God also in this world. Atheists, however, she humanely invites to consider the Gospel of Christ with open heart. For the Church eminently knows that her message concords with the most secret desires of the human heart, since she vindicates the dignity of the human calling, restores hope to those who already despair of their higher lot. Her message, far from diminishing man, pours out light, life, and freedom for his progress; and besides it nothing is able to satisfy the heart of man: "You have made us for yourself, O Lord, and our heart is restless until it rests in you."
¶412
De habitudine Ecclesiae ad atheismum
On the attitude of the Church toward atheism
Ecclesia, fideliter tum Deo tum hominibus addicta, desistere non potest quin dolenter perniciosas illas doctrinas actionesque, quae rationi et communi experientiae humanae contradicunt hominemque ab innata eius excellentia deiiciunt, omni firmitate reprobet, sicut antehac reprobavit. Abditas tamen in atheorum mente negationis Dei causas deprehendere conatur et, de gravitate quaestionum quas atheismus excitat conscia necnon caritate erga omnes homines ducta, eas serio ac profundiori examini subiiciendas esse censet. Tenet Ecclesia agnitionem Dei dignitati hominis nequaquam opponi, cum huiusmodi dignitas in ipso Deo fundetur et perficiatur: homo enim a Deo creante intelligens ac liber in societate constituitur; sed praesertim ad ipsam Dei communionem ut filius vocatur et ad Ipsius felicitatem participandam. Docet praeterea per spem eschatologicam momentum munerum terrestrium non minui, sed potius eorum adimpletionem novis motivis fulciri. Deficientibus e contra fundamento divino et spe vitae aeternae, hominis dignitas gravissime laeditur, ut saepe hodie constat, atque vitae et mortis, culpae et doloris aenigmata sine solutione manent, ita ut homines in desperationem non raro deiiciantur. Omnis homo interea sibi ipsi remanet quaestio insoluta, subobscure percepta. Nemo enim quibusdam momentis, praecipue in maioribus vitae eventibus, praefatam interrogationem omnino effugere valet. Cui quaestioni solus Deus plene et omni certitudine responsum affert, qui ad altiorem cogitationem et humiliorem inquisitionem hominem vocat. Remedium autem atheismo afferendum, cum a doctrina apte exposita, tum ab integra Ecclesiae eiusque membrorum vita exspectandum est. Ecclesiae enim est Deum Patrem eiusque Filium incarnatum praesentem et quasi visibilem reddere, ductu Spiritus Sancti sese indesinenter renovando et purificando. Id imprimis obtinetur testimonio fidei vivae et maturae, ad hoc scilicet educatae ut difficultates lucide perspicere valeat easque superare. Huius fidei testimonium praeclarum plurimi martyres reddiderunt et reddunt. Quae fides suam fecunditatem manifestare debet, credentium integram vitam, etiam profanam, penetrando, eosque ad iustitiam et amorem, praesertim erga egentes, movendo. Ad praesentiam Dei manifestandam maxime denique confert caritas fraterna fidelium, qui spiritu unanimes collaborant fidei Evangelii, et signum unitatis se exhibent. Ecclesia vero, etsi atheismum omnino reiicit, sincere tamen profitetur homines omnes, credentes et non credentes, ad hunc mundum, in quo communiter vivunt, recte aedificandum opem conferre debere: quod certe fieri non potest sine sincero et prudenti colloquio. Conqueritur igitur de discrimine inter credentes et non credentes, quod quidam civitatum rectores, personae humanae iura fundamentalia non agnoscentes, iniuste inducunt. Pro credentibus vero actuosam libertatem expostulat ut in hoc mundo etiam Dei templum exstruere sinantur. Atheos autem humaniter invitat ut Evangelium Christi corde aperto considerent. Apprime etenim novit Ecclesia nuntium suum cum secretissimis humani cordis desideriis concordare, cum vocationis humanae dignitatem vindicat, illis qui iam de altiore sua sorte desperant spem restituens. Nuntium eius, nedum hominem minuat, lucem, vitam et libertatem ad eius profectum fundit; atque praeter illud nihil cordi hominis satisfacere valet: Fecisti nos ad Te, Domine, et inquietum est cor nostrum, donec requiescat in Te.
22 Indeed, only in the mystery of the incarnate Word does the mystery of man truly become clear. For Adam, the first man, was a figure of the future, namely of Christ the Lord. Christ, the new Adam, in the very revelation of the mystery of the Father and His love, fully manifests man to man himself and lays open his most high calling. Nothing therefore is wonderful that in Him the aforesaid truths find their source and attain their summit. He who is the image of the invisible God (Col 1:15), He is the perfect man, who restores the divine likeness to the sons of Adam, deformed from the first sin. Since in Him human nature was assumed, not destroyed, by that very fact it has been raised to a sublime dignity also in us. For He, the Son of God, by His incarnation joined Himself in a certain way with every man. He worked with human hands, thought with a human mind, acted with a human will, loved with a human heart. Born of the Virgin Mary, truly made one of us, He was in all things like to us, sin excepted. The innocent Lamb, by His freely poured out blood, merited life for us, and in Him God reconciled us to Himself and among ourselves, and snatched us from the slavery of the devil and sin, so that each of us can say with the Apostle: "The Son of God loved me and delivered Himself up for me" (Gal 2:20). By suffering for us, He not only gave an example that we should follow His footprints, but also restored the way, which if we follow, life and death are sanctified and receive a new meaning. The Christian man, however, made conformed to the image of the Son who is the Firstborn among many brothers, receives the first-fruits of the Spirit (Rom 8:23), by which he becomes capable of fulfilling the new law of love. Through this Spirit, who is the pledge of inheritance (Eph 1:14), the whole man is inwardly restored, to the redemption of the body (Rom 8:23): "If the Spirit of Him who raised Jesus from the dead dwells in you, He who raised Jesus Christ from the dead shall also quicken your mortal bodies, because of His Spirit dwelling in you" (Rom 8:11). The Christian is certainly urged by necessity and duty to struggle against evil through many tribulations and to suffer death; but associated with the paschal mystery, configured to the death of Christ, he will meet death strengthened by hope of resurrection. Which holds good not only for Christ's faithful, but also for all men of good will in whose heart grace works in an invisible way. For since Christ died for all and the ultimate vocation of man is truly one, namely divine, we must hold that the Holy Spirit offers to all the possibility that, in a way known to God, they may be associated with this paschal mystery. Such and so great is the mystery of man, which is illuminated for believers through Christian Revelation. Through Christ and in Christ, therefore, the enigma of pain and death is illuminated, which apart from His Gospel overwhelms us. Christ rose, by His death destroying death, and gave life to us, that, as sons in the Son, we may cry out in the Spirit: Abba, Father!

De Christo Novo Homine
On Christ the New Man
Reapse nonnisi in mysterio Verbi incarnati mysterium hominis vere clarescit. Adam enim, primus homo, erat figura futuri, scilicet Christi Domini. Christus, novissimus Adam, in ipsa revelatione mysterii Patris Eiusque amoris, hominem ipsi homini plene manifestat eique altissimam eius vocationem patefacit. Nil igitur mirum in Eo praedictas veritates suum invenire fontem atque attingere fastigium. Qui est imago Dei invisibilis (Col 1,15), Ipse est homo perfectus, qui Adae filiis similitudinem divinam, inde a primo peccato deformatam, restituit. Cum in Eo natura humana assumpta, non perempta sit, eo ipso etiam in nobis ad sublimem dignitatem evecta est. Ipse enim, Filius Dei, incarnatione sua cum omni homine quodammodo Se univit. Humanis manibus opus fecit, humana mente cogitavit, humana voluntate egit, humano corde dilexit. Natus de Maria Virgine, vere unus ex nostris factus est, in omnibus nobis similis excepto peccato. Agnus innocens, sanguine suo libere effuso, vitam nobis meruit, in Ipsoque Deus nos Sibi et inter nos reconciliavit et a servitute diaboli ac peccati eripuit, ita ut unusquisque nostrum cum Apostolo dicere possit: Filius Dei dilexit me et tradidit semetipsum pro me (Ga 2,20). Pro nobis patiendo non solummodo exemplum praebuit ut sequamur vestigia Eius, sed et viam instauravit, quam dum sequimur, vita et mors sanctificantur novumque sensum accipiunt. Christianus autem homo, conformis imagini Filii factus qui est Primogenitus in multis fratribus, primitias Spiritus (Rm 8,23) accipit, quibus capax fit legem novam amoris adimplendi. Per hunc Spiritum, qui est pignus hereditatis (Ep 1,14), totus homo interius restauratur, usque ad redemptionem corporis (Rm 8,23): Si Spiritus Eius, qui suscitavit Iesum a mortuis, habitat in vobis: qui suscitavit Iesum Christum a mortuis, vivificabit et mortalia corpora vestra, propter inhabitantem Spiritum eius in vobis (Rm 8,11). Christianum certe urgent necessitas et officium contra malum per multas tribulationes certandi necnon mortem patiendi; sed mysterio paschali consociatus, Christi morti configuratus, ad resurrectionem spe roboratus occurret. Quod non tantum pro christifidelibus valet, sed et pro omnibus hominibus bonae voluntatis in quorum corde gratia invisibili modo operatur. Cum enim pro omnibus mortuus sit Christus cumque vocatio hominis ultima revera una sit, scilicet divina, tenere debemus Spiritum Sanctum cunctis possibilitatem offerre ut, modo Deo cognito, huic paschali mysterio consocientur. Tale et tantum est hominis mysterium, quod per Revelationem christianam credentibus illucescit. Per Christum et in Christo, igitur, illuminatur aenigma doloris et mortis, quod extra Eius Evangelium nos obruit. Christus resurrexit, morte sua mortem destruens, vitamque nobis largitus est ut, filii in Filio, clamemus in Spiritu: Abba, Pater!
23 Among the principal aspects of the modern world, the multiplication of mutual relations among men is to be numbered, to whose development modern technical progress contributes very much. Yet the fraternal conversation of men is perfected not in those progresses, but more deeply in the community of persons, which demands mutual reverence toward their full spiritual dignity. To promote this communion among persons, Christian Revelation brings great aid, and at the same time leads us to a deeper understanding of the higher laws of social life which the Creator inscribed in the spiritual and moral nature of man. Since, however, the more recent documents of the Magisterium of the Church have more fully set forth the Christian doctrine concerning human society, the Council intends only to recall certain principal truths and to set forth their foundations under the light of Revelation. Then it insists on certain consequences which are of greater importance in our days.

CAPUT II. DE HOMINUM COMMUNITATE
CHAPTER II. ON THE COMMUNITY OF MEN
What the Council intends
Quid Concilium intendat
Inter praecipuos mundi hodierni aspectus, mutuarum inter homines necessitudinum multiplicatio adnumeratur, ad quam evolvendam hodierni technici progressus plurimum conferunt. Tamen fraternum hominum colloquium non in istis progressibus, sed profundius in personarum communitate perficitur, quae mutuam reverentiam erga plenam earum dignitatem spiritualem exigit. Ad hanc vero communionem inter personas promovendam, Revelatio christiana magnum subsidium affert, simulque ad altiorem vitae socialis legum intelligentiam nos perducit quas Creator in natura spirituali ac morali hominis inscripsit. Quoniam autem recentiora Ecclesiae Magisterii documenta christianam de societate humana doctrinam fusius exposuerunt, Concilium quasdam tantum principaliores veritates in memoriam revocat earumque fundamenta sub luce Revelationis exponit. Deinde in quaedam consectaria insistit quae nostris diebus maioris sunt momenti.
24 God, who has fatherly care for all, willed that all men should form one family and treat one another with fraternal spirit. For all, created to the image of God, "who made from one every race of men to dwell upon the whole face of the earth" (Acts 17:26), are called to one and the same end, that is to God Himself. Wherefore the love of God and neighbor is the first and greatest commandment. But from sacred Scripture we are taught that the love of God cannot be separated from the love of neighbor: "if there be any other commandment, it is summed up in this word: You shall love your neighbor as yourself... Therefore the fullness of the law is love" (Rom 13:9-10; cf. 1 Jn 4:20). Which is judged to be of the greatest importance for men more and more dependent on one another and for the world more and more unified. Indeed, the Lord Jesus, when He prays the Father that all may be one... even as we are one (Jn 17:21-22), opening up prospects impervious to human reason, suggests a certain likeness between the union of the divine persons and the union of the sons of God in truth and charity. This likeness manifests that man, who is the only creature on earth that God willed for its own sake, cannot fully find himself except by a sincere gift of himself.
¶415
De indole communitaria vocationis humanae in consilio Dei
On the communitarian nature of the human vocation in the design of God
Deus, qui paternam curam omnium habet, voluit ut cuncti homines unam efficerent familiam fraternoque animo se invicem tractarent. Omnes enim creati ad imaginem Dei, qui fecit ex uno omne genus hominum inhabitare super universam faciem terrae (Ac 17,26), ad unum eumdemque finem, id est ad Deum ipsum, vocantur. Quapropter dilectio Dei et proximi primum et maximum mandatum est. A Sacra autem Scriptura docemur Dei amorem a proximi amore seiungi non posse: ... si quod est aliud mandatum, in hoc verbo instauratur: Diliges proximum tuum sicut teipsum ... Plenitudo ergo legis est dilectio (Rm 13,9-10 cf. 1Jn 4,20). Quod vero hominibus magis in dies ab invicem dependentibus atque mundo magis in dies unificato maximi comprobatur esse momenti. Immo Dominus Iesus, quando Patrem orat ut omnes unum sint..., sicut et nos unum sumus (Jn 17,21-22), prospectus praebens humanae rationi impervios, aliquam similitudinem innuit inter unionem personarum divinarum et unionem filiorum Dei in veritate et caritate. Haec similitudo manifestat hominem, qui in terris sola creatura est quam Deus propter seipsam voluerit, plene seipsum invenire non posse nisi per sincerum sui ipsius donum.
25 From the social nature of man it appears that the progress of the human person and the increase of society itself depend on one another. For the principle, subject, and end of all social institutions is and ought to be the human person, who by his very nature wholly needs social life. Since therefore social life is not something extrinsic to man, it follows that man grows and can respond to his vocation through commerce with others, mutual duties, conversation with brothers, with respect to all his gifts. From the social bonds which are necessary for the development of man, some, such as the family and political community, more immediately correspond to his intimate nature; others rather proceed from his free will. In our age, for various reasons, mutual relations and interdependencies increasingly multiply; whence arise various associations and institutions, whether public or private. This fact, however, which is called socialization, although not without its perils, nevertheless carries with it many benefits for confirming and increasing the qualities of the human person and protecting his rights. But if the human person receives much from this social life to fulfill his vocation, even religious, it cannot be denied that men from the social conditions in which they live and, from infancy, are immersed, are often turned away from doing good and impelled to evil. It is certain that the disturbances, so frequently occurring in the social order, partly arise from the tension of economic, political, and social forms themselves. But more deeply they arise from the pride and selfishness of men, which also pervert the social environment. Where the order of things is affected by the sequel of sin, man, born prone to evil, finds new incitements to sin, which, without strenuous efforts helped by grace, cannot be overcome.
¶416
De interdependentia humanae personae et humanae societatis
On the interdependence of the human person and human society
Ex sociali hominis indole apparet humanae personae profectum et ipsius societatis incrementum ab invicem pendere. Etenim principium, subiectus et finis omnium institutorum socialium est et esse debet humana persona, quippe quae, suapte natura, vita sociali omnino indigeat. Cum igitur vita socialis non sit homini quid adventicium, ideo commercio cum aliis, mutuis officiis, colloquio cum fratribus, quoad omnes suas dotes grandescit homo, et suae vocationi respondere potest. Ex socialibus vinculis, quae homini excolendo necessaria sunt, alia, uti familia et communitas politica, intimae eius naturae immediatius congruunt; alia potius ex eius libera voluntate procedunt. Nostra hac aetate, variis de causis, mutuae necessitudines et interdependentiae in dies multiplicantur; unde diversa oriuntur consociationes et instituta sive publici sive privati iuris. Hoc autem factum, quod socializatio nuncupatur, licet periculis sane non careat, multa tamen secum emolumenta affert ad confirmandas et augendas humanae personae qualitates eiusque iura tuenda. Sed si personae humanae ad suam vocationem adimplendam, etiam religiosam, ex hac vita sociali multum accipiunt, negari tamen nequit homines ex adiunctis socialibus in quibus vivunt et, inde ab infantia, immerguntur, saepe a bono faciendo averti et ad malum impelli. Certum est perturbationes, tam frequenter in ordine sociali occurrentes, ex ipsa formarum oeconomicarum, politicarum et socialium tensione pro parte provenire. Sed penitius ex hominum superbia et egoismo oriuntur, quae etiam ambitum socialem pervertunt. Ubi autem ordo rerum sequelis peccati afficitur, homo, proclivis ad malum natus, nova deinde ad peccatum incitamenta invenit, quae, sine strenuis gratia adiuvante conatibus, superari nequeunt.
26 From the ever stricter interdependence and gradually diffused to the whole world, it follows that the common good—that is, the sum of those conditions of social life which permit both groups and individual members to more fully and conveniently attain their own perfection—today more and more becomes universal, and therefore implies rights and duties which respect the whole human race. Every group should have regard for the necessities and legitimate aspirations of other groups, indeed of the common good of the whole human family. At the same time, however, the consciousness grows of the eminent dignity which belongs to the human person, since he excels all things, and his rights and duties are universal and inviolable. Therefore all those things must be made accessible to man which he needs to lead a truly human life, such as food, clothing, habitation, the right freely to choose a state of life and to found a family, to education, to work, to good reputation, to respect, to suitable information, to acting according to the right norm of his conscience, to the protection of private life, and to just freedom even in religious matters. The social order and its progress, therefore, must unceasingly give way to the good of persons, since the ordering of things must be subjected to the order of persons and not the contrary, the Lord Himself intimating this when He said the sabbath was made for man, and not man for the sabbath. That order, to be developed day by day, must be founded in truth, built up in justice, made alive by love; in freedom, however, it must find an ever more human balance. To accomplish these things, the renewal of mind and vast changes in society are to be introduced. The Spirit of God, who by wonderful providence directs the course of times and renews the face of the earth, is present to this development. The evangelical leaven, however, has aroused and arouses in the heart of man the irrepressible exigency of dignity.
¶417
De bono communi promovendo
On promoting the common good
Ex interdependentia in dies strictiore et paulatim ad mundum universum diffusa sequitur bonum commune - seu summam eorum vitae socialis condicionum quae tum coetibus, tum singulis membris permittunt ut propriam perfectionem plenius atque expeditius consequantur - hodie magis magisque universale evadere, et exinde iura officiaque implicare, quae totum humanum genus respiciunt. Quilibet coetus necessitatum et legitimarum appetitionum aliorum coetuum, immo boni communis totius familiae humanae, rationem habere debet. Simul vero conscientia crescit eximiae dignitatis quae personae humanae competit, cum ipsa rebus omnibus praestet, et eius iura officiaque universalia sint atque inviolabilia. Oportet ergo ut ea omnia homini pervia reddantur, quibus ad vitam vere humanam gerendam indiget, ut sunt victus, vestitus, habitatio, ius ad statum vitae libere eligendum et ad familiam condendam, ad educationem, ad laborem, ad bonam famam, ad reverentiam, ad congruam informationem, ad agendum iuxta rectam suae conscientiae normam, ad vitae privatae protectionem atque ad iustam libertatem etiam in re religiosa. Ordo socialis igitur eiusque progressus in bonum personarum indesinenter cedere debent, siquidem rerum ordinatio ordini personarum subiicienda est et non e converso, ipso Domino id innuente cum dixerit sabbatum propter hominem factum esse et non hominem propter sabbatum. Ordo ille in dies evolvendus, in veritate fundandus, in iustitia aedificandus, amore vivificandus est; in libertate autem aequilibrium in dies humanius invenire debet. Ad haec autem implenda mentis renovatio atque amplae societatis immutationes inducendae sunt. Spiritus Dei, qui mirabili providentia temporum cursum dirigit et faciem terrae renovat, huic evolutioni adest. Evangelicum autem fermentum in corde hominis irrefrenabilem dignitatis exigentiam excitavit atque excitat.
27 Descending to practical and more urgent consequences, the Council inculcates reverence toward man, that each should consider his neighbor, no one excepted, as another self, having regard first of all for his life and the means necessary for living it worthily, lest he imitate the rich man who had no care for poor Lazarus. Especially in our times the obligation presses to make ourselves the neighbor of absolutely any man, and to actively serve him when he occurs, whether he be an old man abandoned by all, or a foreign worker unjustly despised, or an exile, or a child born of an illegitimate union, undeservedly suffering for a sin not committed by him, or a hungry man who calls our conscience, recalling the Lord's voice: "As long as you did it to one of these my least brethren, you did it to me" (Mt 25:40). Moreover, whatever things oppose life itself, such as any kind of homicides, genocides, abortion, euthanasia, and voluntary suicide itself; whatever violate the integrity of the human person, such as mutilations, torments inflicted on body or mind, attempts to coerce minds themselves; whatever offend human dignity, such as inhuman living conditions, arbitrary imprisonments, deportations, slavery, prostitution, the commerce of women and young persons; also dishonorable working conditions, in which workers are treated as mere instruments of profit, not as free and responsible persons: all these and other such things are indeed shameful, and while they infect human civilization, they more stain those who so conduct themselves than those who suffer injury, and most greatly contradict the honor of the Creator.
¶418
De reverentia erga personam humanam
On reverence toward the human person
Ad practica urgentioraque consectaria descendens, Concilium reverentiam inculcat erga hominem, ita ut singuli proximum, nullo excepto, tamquam alterum seipsum considerare debeant, de eius vita et de mediis ad illam digne degendam necessariis rationem imprimis habentes, ne divitem illum imitentur, qui pauperis Lazari nullam curam egit. Nostris praesertim diebus urget obligatio nosmetipsos cuiuslibet omnino hominis proximos efficiendi et illi occurrenti actuose inserviendi, sive sit senex ab omnibus derelictus, sive alienigena operarius iniuste despectus, sive exsul, sive infans ex illegitima unione natus, immerito patiens propter peccatum a se non commissum, vel esuriens qui conscientiam nostram interpellat Domini vocem revocans: Quamdiu fecistis uni ex his fratribus meis minimis? mihi fecistis (Mt 25,40). Quaecumque insuper ipsi vitae adversantur, ut cuiusvis generis homicidia, genocidia, abortus, euthanasia et ipsum voluntarium suicidium; quaecumque humanae personae integritatem violant, ut mutilationes, tormenta corpori mentive inflicta, conatus ipsos animos coercendi; quaecumque humanam dignitatem offendunt, ut infrahumanae vivendi condiciones, arbitrariae incarcerationes, deportationes, servitus, prostitutio, mercatus mulierum et iuvenum; condiciones quoque laboris ignominiosae, quibus operarii ut mera quaestus instrumenta, non ut liberae et responsabiles personae tractantur: haec omnia et alia huiusmodi probra quidem sunt, ac dum civilizationem humanam inficiunt, magis eos inquinant qui sic se gerunt, quam eos qui iniuriam patiuntur et Creatoris honori maxime contradicunt.
28 Reverence and charity ought also to be extended to those who think or act otherwise than we do in social, political, or even religious matters; by how much more indeed we more intimately understand their ways of thinking with humanity and charity, by so much the more easily we will be able to enter into dialogue with them. This charity and benignity, of course, ought by no means to render us indifferent toward truth and good. Indeed, charity itself urges the disciples of Christ to announce the saving truth to all men. But we must distinguish between error, always to be rejected, and the erring person, who always retains the dignity of the human person, even when infected with false or less accurate religious notions. God alone is the judge and searcher of hearts: whence He forbids us to judge concerning the interior guilt of anyone. The doctrine of Christ, which also demands that we forgive injuries, extends the precept of love to all enemies, which is the commandment of the New Law: "You have heard that it was said: You shall love your neighbor, and hate your enemy. But I say to you: Love your enemies, do good to those who hate you, and pray for those who persecute and calumniate you" (Mt 5:43-44).
¶419
De reverentia et amore erga adversarios
On reverence and love toward adversaries
Ad illos etiam qui in rebus socialibus, politicis vel etiam religiosis aliter ac nos sentiunt aut faciunt, reverentia et caritas extendi debent; quo magis quidem humanitate et caritate modos sentiendi eorum intimius comprehendemus, eo facilius cum ipsis colloquium inire poterimus. Haec sane caritas et benignitas nequaquam indifferentes erga veritatem et bonum nos reddere debent. Immo caritas ipsa discipulos Christi urget ad veritatem salutarem omnibus hominibus annuntiandam. Sed distinguere oportet inter errorem, semper reiciendum, et errantem, qui dignitatem personae iugiter servat, etiam ubi falsis minusve accuratis notionibus religiosis inquinatur. Deus solus iudex est et scrutator cordium: unde nos vetat de interiore cuiusvis culpa iudicare. Doctrina Christi ut etiam iniuriis ignoscamus postulat praeceptumque amoris ad inimicos omnes extendit, quod est Novae Legis mandatum: Audistis quia dictum est: Diliges proximum tuum, et odio habebis inimicum tuum. Ego autem dico vobis: Diligite inimicos vestros, benefacite his qui oderunt vos: et orate pro persequentibus et calumniantibus vos (Mt 5,43-44).
29 Since all men, possessing a rational soul and created to the image of God, have the same nature and same origin, and, being redeemed by Christ, enjoy the same vocation and divine destiny, fundamental equality among all is more and more to be recognized. Indeed, by various physical capacities and diversity of intellectual and moral forces, not all men are equal. Yet every mode of discrimination in the fundamental rights of the person, whether social or cultural, on account of sex, race, color, social condition, language, or religion, is to be overcome and removed, as contrary to the purpose of God. For it is truly to be mourned that those fundamental rights of the person are not yet everywhere kept whole. As if woman is denied the faculty of freely choosing a spouse and embracing a state of life, or of access to equal education and culture which is acknowledged to man. Moreover, although just diversities exist among men, the equal dignity of persons demands that a more humane and just condition of life be attained. For excessive economic and social inequalities among members or peoples of one human family are scandalous, and oppose social justice, equity, the dignity of the human person, as well as social and international peace. Human institutions, however, whether private or public, should strive to serve the dignity and end of man, at the same time strenuously fighting against any servitude both social and political, and preserving the fundamental rights of man under any political regime. Indeed, such institutions must gradually be congruous with spiritual things, the highest of all, even if sometimes a rather long time is needed to attain the desired end.
¶420
De essentiali inter omnes homines aequalitate et de iustitia sociali
On the essential equality among all men and on social justice
Cum omnes homines, anima rationali pollentes et ad imaginem Dei creati, eamdem naturam eamdemque originem habeant, cumque, a Christo redempti, eadem vocatione et destinatione divina fruantur, fundamentalis aequalitas inter omnes magis magisque agnoscenda est. Sane varia capacitate physica viriumque intellectualium et moralium diversitate non omnes homines aequiparantur. Omnis tamen discriminandi modus in iuribus personae fundamentalibus, sive socialis sive culturalis, ob sexum, stirpem, colorem, socialem condicionem, linguam aut religionem, superandus et removendus est, utpote Dei proposito contrarius. Vere enim dolendum est iura illa fundamentalia personae adhuc non ubique sarta tecta servari. Ut si mulieri denegetur facultas libere sponsum eligendi et vitae statum amplectendi, vel ad parem educationem et culturam quae viro agnoscitur accedendi. Insuper, quamquam inter homines iustae diversitates adsunt, aequalis personarum dignitas postulat ut ad humaniorem et aequam vitae condicionem deveniatur. Etenim nimiae inter membra vel populos unius familiae humanae inaequalitate oeconomicae et sociales scandalum movent, atque iustitiae sociali, aequitati, personae humanae dignitati, necnon paci sociali et internationali adversantur. Humanae autem institutiones, sive privatae sive publicae, dignitati ac fini hominis subservire nitantur, simul adversus quamlibet servitutem tum socialem tum politicam strenue decertantes, et iura hominum fundamentalia sub omni regimine politico servantes. Immo, huiusmodi institutiones spiritualibus rebus, omnium altissimis, paulatim congruant oportet, etiamsi interdum sat longo tempore opus sit ut ad optatum finem perveniant.
30 The profound and rapid change of things more urgently demands that no one, not attending to the course of things or torpid with inertia, indulge in a merely individualistic ethics. The duty of justice and charity is more and more fulfilled by this, that each, contributing to the common good according to his capacities and the needs of others, also promotes and helps institutions, whether public or private, which serve to better the living conditions of men. But there are those who, professing generous and more noble opinions, yet so always live in reality as if they had no care for the necessities of society. Indeed, many, in various regions, make little of social laws and prescriptions. Not a few, by various frauds and deceits, do not fear to evade just taxes or other things due to society. Others set little value on certain norms of social life, for example, concerning health, or concerning the regulation of vehicular traffic, not noticing that by such negligence they bring peril to their own life and that of others. It be sacred to all to reckon social relations among the principal duties of modern man, and to observe them. For the more the world is unified, the more openly the duties of men surpass particular groups and gradually extend to the whole world. Which cannot be done unless individual men and their groups cultivate moral and social virtues in themselves and diffuse them in society, that they may truly become new men and artificers of a new humanity, with the necessary help of divine grace.
¶421
Quod ultra individualisticam ethicam progrediendum sit
That we must progress beyond individualistic ethics
Profunda et velox rerum immutatio urgentius postulat ut nemo sit qui, ad rerum cursum non attendens vel inertia torpens, ethicae mere individualisticae indulgeat. Iustitiae ac caritatis officium magis ac magis adimpletur per hoc quod unusquisque, ad bonum commune iuxta proprias capacitates et aliorum necessitates conferens, etiam institutiones sive publicas sive privatas promovet et adiuvat quae hominum vitae condicionibus in melius mutandis inserviunt. Sunt autem qui, largas generosioresque opiniones profitentes, ita tamen semper reapse vivunt ac si nullam societatis necessitatum curam habeant. Immo, plures, in variis regionibus, leges et praescriptiones sociales minimi faciunt. Non pauci, variis fraudibus ac dolis, iusta vectigalia vel alia quae societati debentur effugere non verentur. Alii normas quasdam vitae socialis, e. gr., ad valetudinem tuendam, aut ad vehiculorum ductum moderandum statutas, parvi aestimant, non animadvertentes se tali incuria vitae suae et aliorum periculum inferre. Sanctum sit omnibus necessitudines sociales inter praecipua hominis hodierni officia recensere easque observare. Quo magis enim mundus unitur, eo apertius hominum munera particulares coetus superant et ad universum mundum paulatim extenduntur. Quod fieri nequit nisi et singuli homines et ipsorum coetus virtutes morales et sociales in seipsis colant et in societate diffundant, ita ut vere novi homines et artifices novae humanitatis exsistant cum necessario auxilio divinae gratiae.
31 So that individual men more accurately fulfill the duty of their conscience both toward themselves and toward the various groups of which they are members, they must be diligently educated to a wider culture of mind, by using the immense aids which are available today to the human race. First of all, the education of the young of whatever social origin is to be so instituted that men and women may be raised up who are not only of cultivated mind but also of great spirit, as being urgently demanded by our time. But man can scarcely reach this sense of responsibility, unless the conditions of life permit him to become conscious of his dignity and to respond to his vocation by spending himself for God and for others. Human freedom, however, often becomes weaker when man falls into extreme poverty, just as it becomes vile when he, indulging in too great facilities of life, encloses himself in a golden solitude. On the contrary, it is strengthened when man accepts the inevitable necessities of social life, assumes the many forms of human association, and obliges himself to the service of the human community. Therefore the will of all to assume their part in common undertakings is to be stimulated. That manner of acting of nations is to be praised, in which the largest part of citizens shares in the public affairs in true freedom. Yet regard must be had for the real condition of each people and the necessary vigor of public authority. That all citizens may be inclined to participate in the life of the various groups of which the social body consists, it is necessary that in these groups they find goods which attract them and dispose them to the service of others. We may rightly think that the future lot of humanity is placed in the hands of those who are able to hand down to future generations the ways of living and hoping.
¶422
De responsabilitate et participatione
On responsibility and participation
Ut singuli homines suum conscientiae officium accuratius impleant tum erga seipsos, tum erga varios coetus quorum membra sunt, diligenter ad ampliorem animi culturam educandi sunt, ingentibus adhibitis subsidiis quae hodie generi humano praesto sunt. Praeprimis educatio iuvenum cuiuslibet socialis originis ita instituenda est, ut viri mulieresque suscitentur qui non tantum exculti ingenii sed et magni animi sint, utpote qui a nostro tempore vehementer postulentur. Sed ad hunc responsabilitatis sensum homo vix pervenit, nisi vitae condiciones ei permittant ut suae dignitatis conscius fiat, et vocationi suae, seipsum pro Deo et pro aliis impendendo, respondeat. Humana vero libertas saepe debilior fit, ubi homo in extremam incidit egestatem, sicut vilescit, ubi ipse nimiis vitae facilitatibus indulgens, in aurea veluti solitudine seipsum includit. E contra roboratur, cum homo inevitabiles vitae socialis necessitates accipit, multiformes exigentias humanae coniunctionis assumit atque humanae communitatis servitio se obstringit. Ideo omnium exstimulanda est voluntas inceptorum communium suas partes assumendi. Laudanda est autem ratio agendi nationum, in quibus pars quam maxima civium in vera libertate rerum publicarum particeps fit. Ratio tamen habenda est condicionis realis uniuscuiusque gentis et necessarii vigoris publicae auctoritatis. Ut vero omnes cives proni sint ad participandam vitam variorum coetuum, quibus corpus sociale constat, necesse est ut his in coetibus bona inveniant, quae ipsos attrahant eosque ad aliorum servitium disponant. Iure arbitrari possumus futuram humanitatis sortem in illorum manibus reponi, qui posteris generationibus vivendi et sperandi rationes tradere valent.
32 Just as God did not create men to live individually, but to form a social union, so it also pleased Him to sanctify and save men not individually, apart from any mutual connection, but to make them into a people who would acknowledge Him in truth and serve Him holy. From the beginning of the history of salvation, He chose men not only as individuals but as members of a certain community. For God, opening His purpose, called those elect His people (Ex 3:7-12), with whom He moreover made a covenant on Sinai. This communitarian character is perfected and consummated in the work of Jesus Christ. For the Incarnate Word Himself willed to be a partaker of human association. He was present at the wedding of Cana, entered the house of Zacchaeus, ate with publicans and sinners. He revealed the Father's love and the excellent vocation of man, by recalling the most common social things and by using expressions and figures of quite everyday life. He sanctified human relations, especially family ones, from which social relations arise, voluntarily subject to the laws of His country. He willed to lead the life of a worker of His own time and region. In His preaching, He clearly commanded the sons of God to treat one another as brothers. In His prayer, He asked that all His disciples might be one. Indeed, He offered Himself unto death for all, the Redeemer of all. "Greater love than this no one has, that one lay down his life for his friends" (Jn 15:13). He commanded the Apostles to preach the Gospel message to all nations, that the human race might become the family of God, in which the fullness of the law would be love. The Firstborn among many brothers, among all who receive Him by faith and charity, after His death and resurrection, by the gift of His Spirit He instituted a new fraternal communion, namely in His body, which is the Church, in which all, each members of one another, according to the diverse gifts granted, render mutual services to one another. This solidarity is always to be increased, until the day when it will be consummated, and when men, saved by grace, as a family beloved by God and Christ the Brother, will render perfect glory to God.
¶423
Verbum Incarnatum et solidarietas humana
The Incarnate Word and human solidarity
Sicut Deus homines non ad singulatim vivendum, sed ad socialem unionem efformandam creavit, ita Ipsi etiam placuit ... homines non singulatim, quavis mutua connexione seclusa, sanctificare et salvare, sed eos in populum constituere, qui in veritate Ipsum agnosceret Ipsique sancte serviret. Inde ab initio historiae salutis Ipse homines elegit non ut individuos tantum sed ut membra cuiusdam communitatis. Illos enim electos Deus, suum aperiens consilium, vocavit populum suum (Ex 3,7-12), quocum insuper in Sinai foedus pepigit. Quae indoles communitaria opere Iesu Christi perficitur et consummatur. Ipsum enim Verbum incarnatum humanae consortionis particeps esse voluit. Canae nuptiis interfuit, in domum Zachaei descendit, cum publicanis et peccatoribus manducavit. Patris amorem hominumque eximiam vocationem, communissimas res sociales commemorando et locutiones figurasque vitae plane cotidianae adhibendo, revelavit. Necessitudines humanas, imprimis familiares, ex quibus rationes sociales oriuntur, sanctificavit, legibus suae patriae voluntarie subditus. Vitam opificis sui temporis et regionis propriam ducere voluit. In sua praedicatione clare mandavit filiis Dei ut tamquam fratres ad invicem se gererent. In sua oratione rogavit ut omnes discipuli sui unum essent. Immo Ipse usque ad mortem sese pro omnibus obtulit, omnium Redemptor. Maiorem hac dilectionem nemo habet, ut animam suam ponat quis pro amicis suis (Jn 15,13). Apostolos autem iussit praedicare omnibus gentibus nuntium evangelicum ut genus humanum familia Dei fieret, in qua plenitudo legis esset dilectio. Primogenitus in multis fratribus, inter omnes qui Eum fide ac caritate recipiunt, post mortem et resurrectionem suam, dono sui Spiritus novam fraternam communionem instituit, in Corpore scilicet suo, quod est Ecclesia, in quo omnes, inter se invicem membra, secundum dona diversa concessa, mutua sibi praestarent servitia. Quae solidarietas semper augenda erit, usque ad illam diem qua consummabitur, et qua homines, gratia salvati, tamquam familia a Deo et Christo Fratre dilecta, perfectam gloriam Deo praestabunt.
33 By his labor and genius, man has always tried to develop his life more; today, however, especially by means of science and technical art, he has extended and continues to extend his dominion over almost all nature, and with the help especially of the increased means of various commerce among nations, the human family gradually recognizes and constitutes itself as one community in the whole world. Whence it comes about that many goods, which man formerly expected especially from supernal powers, today he procures for himself by his own industry. Before this immense effort, which already pervades the whole human race, many questions arise among men. What is the meaning and value of that laboriousness? How are all these things to be used? Toward what end to be attained do the efforts both of individuals and of societies tend? The Church, which guards the deposit of the word of God, from which principles in the religious and moral order are drawn, although it does not always have a ready answer to every question, wishes to join the light of revelation with the skill of all, that the way may be illuminated, which humanity has recently entered upon.
¶424
CAPUT III. DE HUMANA NAVITATE IN UNIVERSO MUNDO
CHAPTER III. ON HUMAN ACTIVITY IN THE WHOLE WORLD
The problem is set
Ponitur problema
Suo labore atque ingenio homo suam vitam amplius evolvere semper conatus est; hodie autem, praesertim ope scientiae et artis technicae, suum dominium in universam fere naturam dilatavit ac iugiter dilatat, et adiuvantibus imprimis auctis inter nationes multimodi commercii mediis, familia humana paulatim tamquam unam in universo mundo communitatem sese agnoscit atque constituit. Quo fit, ut multa bona, quae olim homo a supernis viribus praesertim exspectabat, hodie iam propria industria sibi procuret. Coram immenso hoc conamine, quod totum humanum genus iam pervadit, multae exsurgunt inter homines interrogationes. Quinam est illius operositatis sensus et valor? Quomodo omnibus his rebus utendum est? Ad quem finem assequendum nisus sive singulorum sive societatum tendunt? Ecclesia, quae depositum verbi Dei custodit, ex quo principia in ordine religioso et morali hauriuntur, quin semper de singulis quaestionibus responsum in promptu habeat, lumen revelationis cum omnium peritia coniungere cupit, ut iter illuminetur, quod humanitas nuper ingressa est.
34 This is certain to believers, that human activity, individual and collective, that great effort by which men throughout the course of ages strive to better the conditions of their life, considered in itself, corresponds to the purpose of God. For man, created to the image of God, received the mandate that, subduing the earth with all things contained in it, and ruling the world in justice and holiness, and, acknowledging God the Creator of all, he should refer himself and the totality of things to Him, so that with all things subject to man, the name of God might be wonderful in all the earth. This also regards the most daily work. For men and women who, while procuring the sustenance of life for themselves and their family, so exercise their activities that they duly serve society, can rightly think that by their labor they are developing the work of the Creator, providing for the goods of their brothers, and contributing by personal industry to the fulfilling of the divine plan in history. Christians, therefore, far from thinking that the works which men have produced by their genius and virtue oppose the power of God, and that the rational creature exists as a rival of the Creator, rather are persuaded that the victories of the human race are a sign of the greatness of God and the fruit of His ineffable plan. But the more human power grows, the more the responsibility of individuals and communities extends. Hence it appears that the Christian message does not turn men away from building the world, nor impel them to neglect the good of their fellows, but rather more strictly obliges them to this duty.
¶425
De valore humanae navitatis
On the value of human activity
Hoc credentibus ratum est, navitatem humanam individualem et collectivam, seu ingens illud conamen, quo homines decursu saeculorum suae vitae condiciones in melius mutare satagunt, in seipso consideratum, Dei proposito respondere. Homo enim, ad imaginem Dei creatus, mandatum accepit ut, terram cum omnibus quae in ea continentur sibi subiciens, mundum in iustitia et sanctitate regeret utque, Deum omnium Creatorem agnoscens, seipsum ac rerum universitatem ad Ipsum referret, ita ut rebus omnibus homini subiectis, admirabile sit nomen Dei in universa terra. Quod etiam opera penitus quotidiana respicit. Viri namque et mulieres qui, dum vitae sustentationem sibi et familiae comparant, navitates suas ita exercent ut societati opportune ministrent, iure existimare possunt se suo labore opus Creatoris evolvere, commodis fratrum suorum consulere, et ad consilium divinum in historia adimplendum personali industria conferre. Christiani itaque, nedum arbitrentur opera, quae homines suo ingenio et virtute pepererunt, Dei potentiae opponi, creaturamque rationalem quasi aemulam Creatoris exsistere, potius persuasum habent humani generis victorias signum esse magnitudinis Dei et fructus ineffabilis Ipsius consilii. Quo magis vero hominum potentia crescit, eo latius ipsorum responsabilitas, sive singulorum sive communitatum extenditur. Unde apparet christiano nuntio homines ab exstruendo mundo non averti, nec ad bonum sui similium negligendum impelli, sed potius officio haec operandi arctius obstringi.
35 Human activity, however, just as it proceeds from man, so it is ordered to man. For man, when he works, not only changes things and society, but also perfects himself. He learns many things, cultivates his faculties, goes beyond himself and above himself. Such growth, if rightly understood, is of greater value than the external riches which can be gathered. Man is more worth for what he is than for what he has. Likewise, all those things which men do to attain greater justice, wider fraternity, and a more humane ordering in social relations, are worth more than technical progress. For these progresses can as it were provide the matter for human promotion, but they by themselves alone by no means bring it to act. Hence this is the norm of human activity, that it be congruous with the divine plan and will, with the genuine good of the human race, and permit the individual man placed in society the cult and fulfillment of his whole vocation.
¶426
De humana navitate ordinanda
On the ordering of human activity
Humana vero navitas, sicut ex homine procedit, ita ad hominem ordinatur. Homo enim, cum operatur, non tantum res et societatem immutat, sed et seipsum perficit. Multa discit, facultates suas excolit, extra se et supra se procedit. Huiusmodi incrementum, si recte intelligatur, maioris pretii est quam externae quae colligi possunt divitiae. Magis valet homo propter id quod est quam propter id quod habet. Pariter, omnia quae homines, ad maiorem iustitiam, ampliorem fraternitatem, humanioremque ordinationem in socialibus necessitudinibus obtinendam agunt, plus quam progressus technici valent. Hi enim progressus quasi materiam humanae promotioni praebere possunt, illam autem per se solos nequaquam ad actum deducunt. Unde haec est humanae navitatis norma, quod iuxta consilium et voluntatem divinam cum genuino humani generis bono congruat, et homini individuo vel in societate posito integrae suae vocationis cultum et impletionem permittat.
36 Many of our contemporaries, however, seem to fear that from a closer connection of human activity and religion, the autonomy of men or societies or sciences may be impeded. If by the autonomy of earthly things we mean that created things and societies themselves enjoy their own laws and values, to be gradually discovered, used, and ordered by man, it is wholly right to demand it: which is demanded not only by the men of our age, but also is congruent with the will of the Creator. For from the very condition of creation, all things are furnished with their own stability, truth, goodness, and their own laws and order, which man ought to respect, acknowledging the proper methods of each science and art. Therefore methodical investigation in all disciplines, if it proceeds in a truly scientific way and according to the norms of morality, never truly opposes faith, because profane things and the things of faith take their origin from the same God. Indeed, he who strives to investigate the hidden things of things with humble and constant spirit, is led as if unconsciously by the hand of God, who sustains all things and makes them to be what they are. Hence we may deplore certain habits of mind, which have not been lacking among Christians themselves at times, on account of a not sufficiently grasped legitimate autonomy of science, and from contentions and controversies arising therefrom, have led many minds to think that faith and science are opposed to each other. But if by the autonomy of temporal things is meant that created things do not depend on God, and that man can so use them that he does not refer them to the Creator, no one who acknowledges God does not feel how false such opinions are. For the creature without the Creator vanishes. Moreover, all believers, of whatever religion, have always heard the voice and manifestation of Him in the discourse of creatures. Indeed, through forgetfulness of God, creation itself is obscured.
¶427
De iusta rerum terrenarum autonomia
On the just autonomy of earthly things
Multi tamen coaevi nostri timere videntur, ne ex arctiore humanae navitatis et religionis coniunctione autonomia hominum vel societatum vel scientiarum impediatur. Si per terrenarum rerum autonomiam intelligimus res creatas et ipsas societates propriis legibus valoribusque gaudere, ab homine gradatim dignoscendis, adhibendis et ordinandis, eamdem exigere omnino fas est: quod non solum postulatur ab hominibus nostrae aetatis, sed etiam cum Creatoris voluntate congruit. Ex ipsa enim creationis condicione res universae propria firmitate, veritate, bonitate propriisque legibus ac ordine instruuntur, quae homo revereri debet, propriis singularum scientiarum artiumve methodis agnitis. Ideo inquisitio methodica in omnibus disciplinis, si modo vere scientifico et iuxta normas morales procedit, numquam fidei revera adversabitur, quia res profanae et res fidei ab eodem Deo originem ducunt. Immo, qui humili et constanti animo abscondita rerum perscrutari conatur, etsi inscius quasi manu Dei ducitur qui, res omnes sustinens, facit ut sint id quod sunt. Hinc deplorare liceat quosdam animi habitus, qui aliquando inter christianos ipsos, ob non satis perspectam legitimam scientiae autonomiam, non defuerunt et, contentionibus controversiisque exinde suscitatis, plurium animos eo perduxerunt ut fidem et scientiam inter se opponi censerent. At si verbis rerum temporalium autonomia intelligitur res creatas a Deo non pendere, eisque hominem sic uti posse ut easdem ad Creatorem non referat, nemo qui Deum agnoscit non sentit quam falsa huiusmodi placita sint. Creatura enim sine Creatore evanescit. Ceterum, omnes credentes, cuiuscumque sint religionis, vocem et manifestationem Eius in creaturarum loquela semper audierunt. Immo, per oblivionem Dei ipsa creatura obscuratur.
37 Sacred Scripture, however, to which the experience of ages consents, teaches the human family that human progress, which is a great good of man, carries with it a great temptation: for the order of values being disturbed and evil mixed with good, individuals and groups consider only their own, not others'. Whence it comes about that the world no longer exists as a space of true fraternity, while the increased power of humanity already threatens to destroy the human race itself. For a hard struggle against the powers of darkness pervades the whole history of men, which, having begun from the origin of the world, will persevere until the last day, the Lord saying. Inserted into this battle, man must continually struggle that he may adhere to good, nor can he attain unity in himself without great efforts, the grace of God helping. Wherefore the Church of Christ, trusting in the counsel of the Creator, while acknowledging that human progress can serve true human happiness, cannot but echo that of the Apostle: "Be not conformed to this world" (Rom 12:2), namely to that spirit of vanity and malice which transforms human activity, ordered to the service of God and man, into an instrument of sin. If therefore anyone asks by what way that misery can be overcome, Christians profess that all man's activities, which daily are endangered through pride and inordinate self-love, are to be purified and brought to perfection by the cross and resurrection of Christ. For man, redeemed by Christ and made a new creature in the Holy Spirit, can and ought to love the things created by God. For he receives them from God and respects and reveres them as flowing from the hand of God. Giving thanks to the Benefactor for them, and using and enjoying creatures in the poverty and freedom of spirit, he is introduced into the true possession of the world, "as having nothing, yet possessing all things" (2 Cor 6:10). "For all things are yours; and you are Christ's; and Christ is God's" (1 Cor 3:22-23).
¶428
De humana navitate a peccato corrupta
On human activity corrupted by sin
Sacra vero Scriptura, cui saeculorum consentit experientia, humanam familiam edocet progressum humanum, qui magnum hominis bonum est, magnam tamen tentationem secumferre: ordine enim valorum turbato et malo cum bono permixto, singuli homines ac coetus solummodo quae propria sunt considerant, non vero aliorum. Quo fit ut mundus non iam spatium verae fraternitatis exsistat, dum aucta humanitatis potentia iam ipsum genus humanum destruere minatur. Universam enim hominum historiam ardua colluctatio contra potestates tenebrarum pervadit, quae inde ab origine mundi incepta, usque ad ultimum diem, dicente Domino, perseverabit. In hanc pugnam insertus, homo ut bono adhaereat iugiter certare debet, nec sine magnis laboribus, Dei gratia adiuvante, in seipso unitatem obtinere valet. Quapropter Ecclesia Christi, Creatoris consilio fidens, dum agnoscit progressum humanum verae hominum felicitati inservire posse, non potest tamen quin illud Apostoli resonare faciat: Nolite conformari huic saeculo (Rm 12,2), iili scilicet vanitatis et malitiae spiritui qui humanam navitatem, ad servitium Dei et hominis ordinatam, in instrumentum peccati transmutat. Si quis ergo quaerit, qua ratione miseria illa superari possit, christiani profitentur, omnes hominis navitates, quae per superbiam et inordinatum sui ipsius amorem cotidie in discrimine versantur, Christi cruce et resurrectione purificandas et ad perfectionem deducendas esse. A Christo enim redemptus et in Spiritu Sancto nova creatura effectus, homo ipsas res a Deo creatas amare potest et debet. A Deo enim illas accipit et quasi de manu Dei fluentes respicit et reveretur. Pro illis Benefactori gratias agens et in paupertate et libertate spiritus creaturis utens ac fruens, in veram mundi possessionem introducitur, tamquam nihil habens et omnia possidens. Omnia enim vestra sunt: vos autem Christi, Christus autem Dei (1Co 3,22-23).
38 For the word of God, through whom all things were made, Himself made flesh and dwelling in the land of men, a perfect man, entered into the history of the world, assuming and recapitulating it in Himself. He reveals to us, since "God is charity" (1 Jn 4:8), and at the same time teaches us the fundamental law of human perfection, and consequently of the transformation of the world, to be the new commandment of love. He therefore makes certain to those who believe in divine charity that the way of love is open to all men and that the effort to restore universal fraternity is not in vain. At the same time, He warns that this charity is not to be sought only in great things, but especially in the ordinary circumstances of life. Suffering death for us sinners, by His example He teaches us that we too must bear the cross which flesh and world impose on the shoulders of those who seek peace and justice. By His resurrection, the Lord is constituted Christ, to whom all power is given in heaven and on earth, and already by the power of His Spirit He works in the hearts of men, not only arousing the desire for the future age, but by that very fact also animating, purifying, and strengthening those generous desires by which the human family strives to render its own life more humane and to subject the whole earth to this end. The gifts of the Spirit, however, are various: while He calls some to give a manifest testimony to the desire for a heavenly dwelling and to keep it vivid in the human family, He calls others to devote themselves to the earthly service of men, by this ministry preparing the matter for the kingdom of heaven. He frees all, however, that, denying their own love and taking up all earthly forces into human life, they may extend themselves to the future, when humanity itself will become an offering acceptable to God. Of which hope, as an earnest and viaticum of the way, the Lord left to His own in that sacrament of faith, in which the elements of nature, cultivated by men, are converted into the glorious Body and Blood, a supper of fraternal communion and a foretaste of the heavenly banquet.
¶429
De humana navitate in paschali mysterio ad perfectionem adducta
On human activity brought to perfection in the paschal mystery
Verbum enim Dei, per quod omnia facta sunt, Ipsum caro factum et in hominum terra habitans, perfectus homo in historiam mundi intravit, eam in Se assumens et recapitulans. Ipse nobis revelat, quoniam Deus caritas est (1Jn 4,8), simulque nos docet legem fundamentalem perfectionis humanae, ac proinde transformationis mundi, novam dilectionis esse mandatum. Eos igitur, qui divinae credunt caritati, certos facit, viam dilectionis omnibus hominibus aperiri et conamen fraternitatem universalem instaurandi non esse inane. Simul monet, hanc caritatem non in solis magnis rebus sectandam esse, sed et imprimis in ordinariis vitae adiunctis. Pro nobis omnibus peccatoribus mortem sustinens, suo exemplo nos docet crucem etiam baiulandam esse, quam caro et mundus pacem et iustitiam sectantium humeris imponunt. Sua resurrectione Dominus constitutus, Christus, cui omnis potestas in caelo et in terra data est, per virtutem Spiritus Sui in cordibus hominum iam operatur, non solum venturi saeculi desiderium suscitans, sed eo ipso illa etiam generosa vota animans, purificans et roborans, quibus familia humana suam ipsius vitam humaniorem reddere et totam terram huic fini subiicere satagit. Diversa autem sunt Spiritus dona: dum alios vocat ut caelestis habitationis desiderio manifestum testimonium reddant illudque in humana familia vividum conservent, alios vocat ut terreno hominum servitio se dedicent, hoc suo ministerio materiam regni caelestis parantes. Omnes tamen liberat ut, proprio amore abnegato omnibusque terrenis viribus in vitam humanam assumptis, ad futura se extendant, quando humanitas ipsa fiet oblatio accepta Deo. Cuius spei arrham et itineris viaticum Dominus suis reliquit in illo sacramento fidei, in quo naturae elementa, ab hominibus exculta, in Corpus et Sanguinem gloriosum convertuntur, coena communionis fraternae et caelestis convivii praelibatione.
39 We do not know the time of the consummation of the earth and humanity, nor do we know the manner of the transformation of the universe. The figure of this world indeed passes away, deformed by sin, but we are taught that God prepares a new dwelling and a new earth in which justice dwells, and whose beatitude will fulfill and surpass all desires of peace which arise in the hearts of men. Then, death being conquered, the sons of God will be raised in Christ, and what was sown in weakness and corruption will put on incorruption; and, charity and its work remaining, all creation, which God created for man, will be freed from the servitude of vanity. We are indeed warned that it profits a man nothing if he gain the whole world but lose himself. Yet the expectation of a new earth ought not to weaken, but rather to excite, the care for cultivating this earth, where the body of that new human family grows, which can already offer some adumbration of the new age. Therefore, although earthly progress must be sedulously distinguished from the increase of the kingdom of Christ, insofar as it can however contribute to better ordering human society, it is of great interest to the kingdom of God. For the goods of human dignity, fraternal communion, and freedom—namely all these goods of nature and of our industry—after we have spread them on earth in the Spirit of the Lord and according to His mandate, we will later find them again, cleansed from all stain, illuminated and transfigured, when Christ hands over the eternal and universal kingdom to the Father: a kingdom of truth and life, a kingdom of holiness and grace, a kingdom of justice, love, and peace. On earth this kingdom is already present in mystery; at the coming of the Lord, however, it will be consummated.
