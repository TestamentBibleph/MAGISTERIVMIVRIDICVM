Justification for the Three-Volume Structure

The division into three volumes is not merely organizational — it reflects three genuinely distinct kinds of magisterial evidence, each answering a different apologetic question, and each requiring a different methodological frame. Collapsing them into a single volume would blur these distinctions in ways that weaken the overall argument.

Volume 1 answers the question of continuity: does the Latin tradition actually hold the same faith as the seven councils universally received by both East and West? This volume is fundamentally comparative — its unit of analysis is the Greek dogma, and its task is to trace that dogma's reception across two independent Latin streams (pre- and post-Gregorian). Its evidentiary structure is therefore necessarily three-tiered (Greek source → Latin pre-Gregorian → Latin post-Gregorian), which is a structure unique to this volume. No other volume could absorb this material without either duplicating it or losing the comparative architecture that makes the continuity argument work. This volume is aimed primarily at the Byzantine objection that the Latin Church has departed from the patristic-conciliar consensus.

Volume 3 answers the question of foundation: on what basis did the Latin Church's distinctive doctrinal and ecclesiological positions develop before it began producing its own ecumenical councils? This is the prehistory — local and regional Latin synods (African, Iberian, Gallic, Roman, Carolingian) that establish the doctrinal vocabulary, the grace theology, the Trinitarian precision, and crucially the pattern of papal initiative (Lateran 649, the 1059 election decree) that the Gregorian reform will later formalize and that Vatican I will ultimately define. Without this volume, Volume 2's claims about papal authority would appear to spring from nowhere in 1123 — Volume 3 supplies the genealogy. This volume is aimed primarily at showing that Latin ecclesiology is not a medieval innovation but has deep pre-Gregorian roots, while honestly acknowledging that these councils were local rather than universal.

Volume 2 answers the question of self-sufficiency: can the Latin Church, considered as its own magisterial tradition from the Gregorian reform onward, justify itself using only its own ecumenical councils? This is the mature, self-standing corpus — the councils that the Latin Church itself recognizes as ecumenical in the fullest sense, exercising the authority whose foundation Volume 3 traced and whose continuity with the universal Church Volume 1 established. This volume is aimed at both Protestant objections (which often target Trent and Vatican I specifically) and at demonstrating that the post-Gregorian Latin magisterium is not merely continuous with the past but constitutes a coherent, internally developing doctrinal system in its own right — including the internal tensions (Constance/Haec Sancta, Vatican II's pastoral character) that a serious presentation cannot suppress.

Why this order, and why three separate volumes rather than one:

The logical sequence is foundation → continuity → self-sufficiency, but the volumes are numbered to mirror how an interlocutor's objections typically arise: first the comparative question (are you the same as the ancient councils?), then the question of how the distinctively Latin elements arose (where does your ecclesiology even come from?), then the question of the mature tradition's internal coherence (does your own magisterium hold together?). Each volume uses a different unit of analysis (dogma / local synod / ecumenical council), a different evidentiary structure (three-tier comparative / regional-chronological / two-section dogmatic-supplementary), and answers to a different category of objector (Byzantine / general historical-ecclesiological / Protestant and internal-Catholic). Fusing them would force one evidentiary structure to do three incompatible jobs — which is precisely the kind of structural imprecision that weakens apologetic work under serious scrutiny.
